# Collecting From the Debtor in the Netherlands

## Bottom line

For a claim of **155,000 PLN**, the principal alone is roughly **€36,600** at the ECB reference rate for **3 June 2026**. At that size, **Netherlands enforcement is economically serious enough to justify action**, but it is not a “set and forget” wage-only case. If the debtor truly has a Dutch employer and a Dutch bank account, the **best default route** is to finish the Polish case, get a **Polish enforceable judgment/order**, and then use **Brussels Ia** with the **Article 53 certificate** for Dutch execution. I would treat **EEO certification** as an optional add-on only if the Polish service history is very clean. I would also seriously consider an **EAPO** if you want to locate and freeze a Dutch bank account before money moves. citeturn45search2turn33view2turn33view0turn20view0turn19view1turn19view4

The main reason is practical rather than theoretical. **Brussels Ia** works for any enforceable Polish civil judgment; it does **not** require you to prove that the claim was “uncontested” in the narrower **EEO** sense. By contrast, **EEO** is stricter: it is only for **uncontested claims**, and the court of origin must be satisfied that the proceedings met the Regulation’s minimum standards on service, information, and review rights. CJEU case law is particularly strict where the debtor’s address was not known with certainty, where the debtor was not properly informed how to respond, or where default service is questionable. That makes EEO the **more attack-proof route once granted**, but also the **more fragile certification route** for an EPU order that became final because the debtor did not respond after possibly fictitious service. citeturn23view0turn22view0turn23view1turn23view2turn25view3turn28view0

For this debtor profile, the money question is simple: **bank first, wages second**. Dutch wage garnishment is real, but the **beslagvrije voet** leaves the debtor with a protected minimum. That means a modest production-line salary can yield only **low hundreds of euros per month** in recoverable cash, and sometimes less if rent, care-cost, family-status, or earlier attachments raise the protected amount. A successful **bank attachment** or **EAPO** can change the economics dramatically; a wage attachment alone may turn into a years-long drip. citeturn40view0turn40view1turn39search1turn41search1turn41search5

## Best route for your EPU title

The route I would recommend for your **EPU nakaz** is this:

First, **finish the Polish case and get a title that is both final and enforceable in Poland**. Once you have that, ask the **court of origin** for the **Brussels Ia Article 53 certificate**. Under Brussels Ia, a judgment enforceable in one Member State is enforceable in another without any declaration of enforceability, and the enforcing authority needs the judgment plus the Article 53 certificate. The Regulation also says you do **not** need a Dutch postal address or local representative merely because you are enforcing in the Netherlands. citeturn33view2turn33view0

Second, if the service history is exceptionally strong, you can **also** ask for **EEO certification** as a parallel option. Under the EEO Regulation, the application is made to the **court of origin**, and it can be made **at any time**; the CJEU has also confirmed that the certification is a **judicial act**. But I would only do that if the papers show one of two things: either the debtor was actually served, or the service method and review rights fit the EEO minimum standards without strain. citeturn22view0turn25view0turn25view3

Third, do **not** build your plan around EEO if the EPU went final by **fiction-of-service** to a Polish address while the debtor was in fact living and working in the Netherlands. EEO Article 14 allows some service methods without proof of receipt, but only where the debtor’s address is **known with certainty**. The CJEU’s line is restrictive: a default judgment cannot be certified as an EEO where the defendant’s address is unknown; a judgment also cannot be certified if the debtor was not informed of the court/address to respond or appeal; and certification requires national law to allow a real, full review in the exceptional situations covered by Article 19. On your facts, that makes **EEO plausible only if the Polish file can withstand a very exact service audit**. citeturn23view1turn23view2turn25view3turn28view0

So the practical recommendation is:

1. **Default path:** use **Brussels Ia** on the final Polish title.  
2. **Optional bonus path:** apply for **EEO** only if the service/review record is clean enough that you would be comfortable litigating that point.  
3. **If the EPU gets derailed** because service/opposition turns into a mess, then a fresh **European Order for Payment** against the known Dutch residence/address may become the cleaner EU-native alternative. citeturn33view2turn34view0turn23view1turn25view3turn29search0

## Freezing Dutch bank accounts

The **EAPO** is available **before** you obtain judgment, **during** the merits proceedings, and **after** you obtain a judgment, settlement, or authentic instrument. Before judgment, the competent court is the court with jurisdiction over the merits; after judgment, jurisdiction lies with the courts of the Member State that issued the judgment. That means a **Polish court can in principle issue an EAPO directed at a Dutch bank account** in support of a Polish claim or Polish judgment. citeturn19view0turn20view0

The evidentiary threshold is meaningful. Under Article 7, the court must be satisfied that there is an **urgent need** because there is a **real risk** that subsequent enforcement would be impeded or made substantially more difficult without the order. If you do **not yet** have judgment, you must also show that you are **likely to succeed on the merits**. In your fact pattern, the combination of a documentary refund claim, known Dutch residence/employment, and a suspected Dutch salary account is the kind of material that can support an application, but the “real risk” showing still has to be made in concrete terms. citeturn20view0

The procedure is **ex parte**. The debtor is not notified of the application and is not heard before the order is issued. That is exactly why it is useful as a freeze measure. It is also why **security** matters: before judgment, the court must generally require the creditor to provide security sufficient to prevent abuse and cover debtor damage, unless it considers security inappropriate in the circumstances; after judgment, security is discretionary. citeturn19view1turn19view2turn19view3

If you do **not know the bank**, Article 14 gives you the EU bank-information mechanism. Ordinarily, that mechanism is available where you already have an **enforceable** judgment, settlement, or authentic instrument and can show you have reason to believe the debtor holds an account in a specific Member State. There is also a narrower possibility even before enforceability if the amount is substantial and there is urgent need for the information. For the Netherlands, the designated account-information authority is the **bailiff**, who can require Dutch banks to reply promptly. citeturn20view1turn46view1

Timing is one of EAPO’s best features. The Regulation says the court should decide within **10 working days** when you do not yet have a judgment, and within **5 working days** when you already do, subject to added time where account information or security is involved. Once issued, the order is recognised and enforceable in the other Member States without a declaration of enforceability, and the authorities involved must act without delay. citeturn19view4turn19view5

On cost, the Polish notification says an EAPO application carries a **PLN 100 court fee** in Poland. On the Dutch side, the e-Justice notification says Dutch banks cannot charge fees for implementing an equivalent national order, and that bailiff fees are governed by the Dutch Bailiffs’ Fees Decree. The Dutch e-Justice page still quoted **2025** court-fee figures for applications of indeterminate value, so I would treat those as **order-of-magnitude only** rather than a reliable 2026 price list. citeturn16view0turn46view1turn46view4

Functionally, yes: **EAPO is the cross-border EU substitute for the bank-freeze you cannot get domestically through the EPU apparatus**. But it is narrower than a general asset freeze. It preserves **bank funds only**. It does not directly garnish wages and it does not by itself produce payment to you; the funds stay preserved until ordinary enforcement takes over. citeturn19view5

## Dutch execution against wages and bank accounts

On the ground, Dutch enforcement is carried out by a **gerechtsdeurwaarder**. A creditor or collection agency cannot themselves seize wages or bank balances; the bailiff must do it, and an enforceable title is normally required. Dutch sources also make clear that wage attachments and other seizures are centrally registered in the **DBR** so that multiple bailiffs can see existing attachments. Under Brussels Ia, you do not need a Dutch address or local representative purely in order to enforce, so there is **no legal necessity for you to appear in person**; in practice, this means you can instruct a Dutch bailiff remotely with the certified documents and translations. citeturn40view2turn40view3turn41search1turn33view0

For **wage garnishment**, the key Dutch concept is the **beslagvrije voet**. Business.gov’s official explanation says the protected earnings rate is **generally at least 95% of the social-assistance standard including holiday pay**, and the bailiff calculates it using official registry and income data. Rijksoverheid also states that **at least 5% of the debtor’s net income goes to creditors**. The current social-assistance norm for a **single adult of working age** is **€1,401.50 per month** from **1 January 2026**, which puts the rough baseline protected floor at about **€1,331 per month** before any upward adjustments for facts like housing and care costs. citeturn40view1turn40view0turn39search1

That is why the economics of wage-only recovery are often disappointing. A production worker is not at the top end of the Dutch wage distribution, and the current statutory minimum wage for adults is **€14.71 per hour in the first half of 2026**. Without knowing his household composition, rent, care costs, net pay, or whether another creditor already got there first, I cannot responsibly give you a firm monthly garnishment number. But the legal structure strongly suggests that a modest wage usually yields **modest monthly recovery**, not a fast full payoff. citeturn38search4turn40view1turn40view0

Using your principal-only amount of about **€36,570**, the recovery horizon from wages alone looks like this:

| Attachable amount per month | Time to collect principal only |
|---|---:|
| €150 | about 20.3 years |
| €300 | about 10.2 years |
| €500 | about 6.1 years |
| €800 | about 3.8 years |

Those are **illustrative math scenarios**, not predictions, but they show the core point: **wages alone may be too slow**, especially once Polish statutory interest and execution costs keep accruing. The real upside in the Netherlands usually comes from a **bank hit** or a combination of bank hit plus wage attachment. citeturn45search2turn40view0turn40view1

As to **bank attachment**, Rijksoverheid confirms that a Dutch bailiff can seize the balance on a bank account, and Dutch law now also provides a **protected reserved amount** on bank attachment so that the debtor is not stripped below subsistence. That means bankbeslag is extremely useful, but not literally “empty the whole account no matter what.” Tactically, it is still often the fastest way to get meaningful money because it can catch accumulated balances, salary just after payday, holiday pay, and similar inflows. citeturn41search1turn41search5

On cost, the important point is that **there is no Dutch exequatur stage to pay for** under either EEO or Brussels Ia. Your front-end Dutch costs are mainly bailiff and translation costs. Public 2026 tariff sheets published by Dutch enforcement businesses show the order of magnitude quite clearly: roughly **€165** to serve a title, about **€224** for **wage attachment**, about **€315** for **bank attachment**, plus information costs, VAT, and any additional steps. I treat those figures as **illustrative market-facing tariff publications**, not as the last word on every file, but they are good enough to show that a first Dutch enforcement package is usually **hundreds of euros, not thousands**, before any lawyer-led dispute. You still need to **front** those costs in practice; whether you recover all of them depends on what assets are actually found and realised. citeturn42search5turn42search11turn33view0

## Economic verdict

My honest view is that **Netherlands enforcement is economically rational for this claim**, but only if you approach it as a **targeted pressure-and-collection campaign**, not as a passive wage deduction file.

Why it is worth doing: the principal is already about **€36.6k**, there is continuing interest, and the EU framework removes exequatur and gives you a workable route into Dutch bailiff execution. The expected front-end spend for certification, translation, and a first round of Dutch attachments is modest compared with the claim size. A single meaningful **bank recovery** can justify the whole exercise. citeturn45search2turn33view2turn33view0turn42search5turn42search11

Why it may still disappoint: if the debtor has **no savings**, only a **modest salary**, possible **earlier creditors**, and a living situation that pushes the **beslagvrije voet** upward, then wage attachment becomes a long-tail collection tool. On those facts, Dutch enforcement is still useful, but it is more valuable as **leverage**, as a means of forcing a serious payment plan, or as a way to intercept bank balances and salary credits, than as a realistic way to liquidate the full claim quickly through wages alone. citeturn40view0turn40view1turn40view3

So my practical verdict is:

**Yes, pursue Dutch enforcement.**  
But do it in this order:

- **Finish the Polish title.**
- Use **Brussels Ia Article 53** as the core route.
- Consider **EEO only if service is pristine**.
- Hit **bank accounts and wages simultaneously** through a Dutch bailiff.
- Use **EAPO** if bank details are unknown or you fear dissipation.
- Treat a wage-only file as **slow leverage**, not a quick recovery engine. citeturn33view2turn34view0turn20view1turn46view1

## Criminal track and limitations

If Polish criminal authorities obtain a **freezing order** under the criminal track, the cross-border instrument to reach Dutch assets is now **Regulation (EU) 2018/1805** on the mutual recognition of **freezing orders and confiscation orders**. It is an authority-to-authority system for criminal matters. The Regulation requires the executing State to act **without delay**, and where immediate freezing is necessary it sets a very fast timetable: recognition within **48 hours** after receipt and concrete execution measures within the next **48 hours** after the recognition decision. In other words, a real Polish criminal freeze can absolutely be projected into the Netherlands. citeturn43search0turn44view2

The important limitation is control. You, as the victim/creditor, do **not** independently trigger Regulation 2018/1805 in the way you trigger Brussels Ia civil enforcement. A Polish **prosecutor or criminal court** must issue and transmit the criminal freeze/confiscation instrument. So if the criminal file matures, your leverage is to press the Polish authorities to use the EU criminal mutual-recognition route against Dutch assets. citeturn43search0turn44view2

For an **Article 46 k.k. restitution/compensation order**, the picture is less clean. Regulation 2018/1805 expressly protects victims’ rights to **compensation and restitution** and gives priority to those interests when frozen or confiscated property is disposed of. But that does **not automatically mean** that every Polish victim-compensation order will function abroad exactly like a civil money judgment in your own hands. In practice, unless the Polish criminal authorities actively use the criminal mutual-recognition machinery in a way that captures the debtor’s Dutch assets, you should assume that the **civil Brussels Ia route remains your reliable fallback for collection**. citeturn44view3turn43search0

### Open questions and limitations

The biggest unresolved issue is the **service history in the Polish EPU**. That single fact will drive both **EEO viability** and the debtor’s ability to attack later enforcement under **Brussels Ia** if the judgment was entered in default. citeturn23view1turn25view3turn33view1

The second unknown is the debtor’s **real Dutch financial profile**: whether he is single or supports a household, whether he rents expensively, whether he already has other attachments, and whether his salary lands in a normal Dutch current account with a meaningful balance on payday. Those facts determine whether recovery is months, years, or mostly leverage. citeturn40view0turn40view1turn40view3

If I had to reduce the whole strategy to one sentence, it would be this: **Use Brussels Ia as the core, use EAPO to catch the Dutch bank account, use wage attachment as a secondary pressure tool, and treat EEO as optional only if your Polish service record is strong enough that you would bet the certification fight on it.**
