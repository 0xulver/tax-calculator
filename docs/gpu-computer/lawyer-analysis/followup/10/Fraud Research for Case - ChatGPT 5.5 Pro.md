## Legal baseline under art. 286 § 1 KK

Art. 286 § 1 KK requires: purpose of obtaining a financial benefit, causing another person to make an adverse disposition of property, and doing so by one of the statutory methods, especially **wprowadzenie w błąd**. ([Isap][2]) The Supreme Court’s 2025 decision in **III KK 216/25** is directly relevant: mere taking of an advance, non-performance, and non-refund is not enough unless the judgment/complaint identifies a statutory deceptive method; SN emphasized that fraud is built on “fałsz” and that the victim’s mistaken image of reality must concern facts material to the property decision. 

For your theory, the key rule is timing. The misleading act must **precede** the property disposition; post-payment lies can corroborate intent but are not themselves the original method of fraud for the 12.01.2026 payment. SN III KK 100/17 states that deceptive acts after the disposition, aimed merely at keeping the money, do not satisfy art. 286 as the causal method.   The Supreme Court also describes “wprowadzenie w błąd” as creating in the disposer an image of **existing reality** different from the real state of affairs, with a causal relation to both the mistaken belief and the adverse disposition. 

On materiality: your subjective statement “I would not have paid had I known” is necessary and useful, but it should be pleaded with objective weight. Courts look for a mistake about facts **material to the decision**; at the same time, the adverse nature of the disposition is assessed from the victim’s standpoint and does not require final economic loss if the victim accepted a materially different risk than intended.    For a 155,000 PLN full prepayment, the real identity/capacity of the operator, existence of a real operating base, and full-value insurance are objectively material.

## Misrepresentation 1 — “real operating Kraków computer business”

**Element fit:** medium as a standalone fraud representation; strong as part of the aggregate “fake front.” A general impression that “this is a genuine business” is broad, but it becomes art. 286-relevant if pleaded as concrete facts: a Kraków operating base, real operational capacity, responsible named operator, and ability to source/build/deliver a custom ML workstation. False statements about current capacity, financial/operational ability, or identity can be present-fact deception rather than a future promise. SN case law accepts fraud where the victim is induced by a false image of existing reality; creating “pozory” that affect a material decision may suffice, and the victim’s lack of caution does not negate fraud. 

**Evidence you say you have:** LinkedIn screenshots showing Mateusz working full-time in the Netherlands since 2022; Waldek acting as de facto operator; GPUcomputer mailboxes operated in practice by Waldek; and the formal JDG/NIP/address structure. Your prompt states these facts and ties them to the operational-impossibility argument.  The current GPUcomputer contact page also publicly lists `waldek@gpucomputer.pl` for configurations and `mateusz@gpucomputer.pl` for hardware, which helps show Waldek was not merely incidental but publicly assigned a sales/configuration role. ([gpucomputer][3])

**Weakness:** full-time employment abroad does not by itself prove fraud. A person can run a mail-order business remotely, use contractors, or delegate operations. This becomes criminally strong only if the investigation shows that the formal owner was a front, the real operator was concealed, there was no capable operating infrastructure, and the front was used to induce a high-value prepayment.

**Gaps to close:** employment confirmation beyond LinkedIn; who controlled the bank account; who negotiated the proforma; who had website/admin/email access; whether any workshop, stock, suppliers, courier account, or insurance existed; and whether Mateusz knowingly permitted Waldek to use the JDG as a front.

## Misrepresentation 2 — “W sklepie prezentujemy wybrane produkty” / store-and-office appearance

**Element fit:** weak-to-medium if based only on “W sklepie prezentujemy wybrane produkty”; stronger if tied to the “Our office” map/address presentation and proof of no real office/presence. The current page’s full context says the shop is only mail-order and there is no personal pickup, then says selected products are presented “in the shop.” That makes “sklep” naturally capable of meaning **online shop**, not physical store. ([gpucomputer][1])

**What remains actionable:** if the Google Maps pin, contact page, or other materials presented Mogilska 16/7 as “our office” or a real Kraków operating presence, while it was only a virtual office and they had no real presence, stock, or operational staff there, that is a more concrete present-fact theory. The GPUcomputer contact page gives Mogilska 16/7 as the contact address and NIP. ([gpucomputer][3]) WorkDesk publicly describes Mogilska 16/7 as a “Biuro wirtualne,” which supports your virtual-office proof. ([workdesk.pl][4])

**Weakness:** a virtual office is not inherently fraudulent. It is common and lawful. The criminal point is not “they used a virtual office”; it is “they allegedly used a virtual office and website/map presentation to create a false appearance of a real operating Kraków merchant materially safer than the actual arrangement.”

**Gaps to close:** the exact site/map content on 12.01.2026; who controlled the Google Business Profile; whether WorkDesk provided only mail handling or any actual office/meeting/stock access; whether any customer could inspect/collect goods; and whether the site’s “only mail-order” wording was present before your payment.

## Misrepresentation 3 — “Każda przesyłka … ubezpieczona do pełnej wartości”

**Element fit:** high. This is the strongest single misrepresentation because it is not puffery. It is a specific, checkable statement about a present business practice: every shipment is packed and insured to full value. The current GPUcomputer shop page contains that wording. ([gpucomputer][1])

**Why it matters under art. 286:** for a 155,000 PLN prepaid workstation, shipment insurance directly affects risk. If there was no full-value insurance arrangement, no eligible courier policy, no shipment, or no claim despite a supposed lost shipment, then the statement supports a false pre-payment security representation. Your later contradictory emails — “customs cleared” versus “Hong Kong supplier went bankrupt and parts never arrived” — would be powerful circumstantial evidence, but the prosecution still needs the primary records. Your prompt describes those contradictions and lack of insurance claim/payout. 

**Weakness:** absence of an insurance payout does not by itself prove no insurance. A claim might be denied, excluded, never triggered because no shipment occurred, or dependent on courier documentation. That is why the complaint should ask the prosecutor to obtain courier, insurer, supplier, customs, tracking, and bank records.

**Gaps to close:** whether the wording was live before 12.01.2026; whether any DPD/UPS/courier label was created; whether declared value matched 155,000 PLN; whether any cargo/shipment policy existed; whether customs clearance actually occurred; whether a supplier invoice/order existed; and who knew the insurance statement was false when the proforma and payment were obtained.

## Aggregate “fake front” theory

This is the best framing. Do not plead three isolated website slogans. Plead that, **before payment**, persons acting under GPUcomputer created a false image of the existing reality: a Polish/Kraków operating merchant with identifiable responsible people, a real business base, selected products offered through its shop, and full-value insured delivery. That image allegedly induced a 155,000 PLN prepayment. This fits the Supreme Court’s requirement that the deception precede the disposition and that it concern material elements of the victim’s decision.  

This aggregate theory is also closer to how e-commerce fraud cases are built evidentially. In one lower-court online-shop fraud case, the court examined the shop website, the listed correspondence address, domain/provider records, platform account data, and bank account records; it convicted on art. 286 where online listings misled buyers as to intent/possession/performance. That is a useful evidence model for your complaint, even though it is not Supreme Court authority. ([Saos][5])

## Puffery / *dolus bonus* boundary

Non-actionable puffery is vague praise: “perfect workstations,” “best quality,” “we gladly help,” “professional service.” Those are not normally concrete present facts.

Actionable deception is different: “each shipment is insured to full value,” “we have stock,” “we have a physical office/store,” “we are the actual seller,” “goods cleared customs today,” or “the supplier shipped/failed” are factual and verifiable. Polish unfair-competition law is not the same as art. 286 KK, but it helps draw the boundary: misleading advertising is advertising that misleads the client and can affect the purchase decision; assessment includes all elements, including quality, manner of performance, usefulness, repair/maintenance, and customer conduct. ([Isap][6])

UOKiK’s consumer guidance on false online shops also treats business origin, hidden dropshipping, and misleading presentation of who the consumer is really buying from as material to internet purchase decisions. That supports your argument that identity, location, and operational reality are not trivial background facts in a high-value e-commerce transaction. ([Urząd Ochrony Konkurencji i Konsumentów][7])

## Fake front, identity/capacity deception, and “firmanctwo”

Misrepresenting the **nature, location, and operational reality** of a business can be art. 286 deception when it creates a false image of the counterparty’s identity and capacity to perform. The more the prosecution can prove that the registered JDG was a front for a non-registered de facto operator, the stronger the criminal picture becomes.

Use “firmanctwo” carefully. Strictly, it is a tax/fiscal concept: using another person’s name/company, with consent, to conceal business activity or its real scale. It is not itself an element of art. 286 KK. But the same facts can strengthen art. 286 as **identity-and-capacity deception**: the buyer thought he was contracting with a real, responsible, registered merchant, while the actual operator and operating capacity were allegedly different or concealed. ([SIP Lex][8])

For attribution, art. 18 § 1 KK matters. It covers not only a person acting alone, but also acting “wspólnie i w porozumieniu,” directing another person, or ordering another person to commit the act through dependency. ([Isap][2]) If Waldek controlled the site/mailboxes and sales representations, he may be treated as a direct perpetrator or co-perpetrator. If Mateusz owned the JDG, accepted/controlled the account, authorized the website, or knowingly allowed Waldek to use the business front, that supports co-perpetration. If one of them lacked knowledge and intent, liability may split: one suspect, one witness, or possible fiscal/civil responsibility only.

## Likelihood assessment

Compared with a concealment-only theory, this materially improves the case.

A bare complaint saying “they took prepayment, did not deliver, promised refund, did not refund” risks being screened as a civil dispute under the logic of SN III KK 216/25. Your better complaint says: “the money was obtained by a defined pre-payment method of deception: false present representations about operating reality, identity/capacity, and full-value insured delivery.” That directly addresses the missing element identified by the Supreme Court.
