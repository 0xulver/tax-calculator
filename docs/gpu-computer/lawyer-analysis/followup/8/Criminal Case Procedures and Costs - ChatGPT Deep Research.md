# Who runs your fraud case and what it costs you in Poland

## Bottom line

For an ordinary fraud case under **art. 286 § 1 KK** like the one you describe, **the prosecutor and Police run the criminal case at State expense**. You do **not** have to hire and pay a lawyer to “run the prosecution.” Under **art. 10 § 1 KPK**, for offenses prosecuted *ex officio* the authority must initiate and conduct the preparatory proceedings, and the public prosecutor must bring and support the accusation. Fraud under **art. 286 § 1 KK** is, as a rule, such a public-prosecution offense. The narrow exception in **art. 286 § 4 KK** applies only when the fraud was committed **against a close person**; that carve-out does not fit a commercial seller-buyer fraud by an unrelated trader. citeturn41view0turn42view0

So the plain answer to your headline question is: **the State runs the case; a private lawyer is optional for most of it**. The important exception is the **subsidiary indictment** backstop: if you get through the refusal/umorzenie path twice and want to go forward yourself under **art. 55 KPK**, that indictment **must** be drafted and signed by an advocate or legal adviser. That is the one stage where counsel is not merely optional. citeturn39view0turn27view1turn27view2

One current procedural nuance matters for your numbers. Because the alleged loss is **155,000 PLN**, the case may procedurally run as a **dochodzenie** rather than a full **śledztwo**: under current **art. 325b § 1 pkt 3 KPK**, *dochodzenie* may be used for **art. 286 § 1 i 2 KK** where the value of the object of the offense or the damage does not exceed **200,000 PLN**. Even so, it remains a **public**, State-run case, normally conducted by the Police under prosecutorial control, and the prosecutor can still take over because of weight or complexity. That does **not** turn it into a private prosecution or shift the investigative burden onto you. citeturn23view1turn23view0turn20view2turn22view0

## Who carries the prosecution burden

Once you file your **zawiadomienie**, the authorities must make a formal decision whether to open or refuse the pre-trial case. Filing the report is **free**. You can file it **yourself**, in writing or orally, and after an oral report you may be questioned immediately as a witness. If the authority needs to do preliminary checking first, the Code gives it a limited checking phase and requires the opening/refusal decision within **30 days** of receiving the report. citeturn18search0turn18search11turn27view2turn22view0

As to “who runs the case,” the Code is explicit. The aim of the preparatory proceedings is to determine whether an offense was committed, detect the perpetrator, identify the injured persons and the scale of damage, and **collect, secure, and preserve evidence for the court**. The preparatory proceedings are led or supervised by the **prosecutor**, and within the statutory scope they are conducted by the **Police**. In a public-prosecution case, this is their burden, not yours. citeturn20view0turn20view2turn41view0turn22view0

That is also why the State can do the evidentiary work that you cannot. Authorities can require institutions and persons to assist the criminal process, compel the production of items and documents that may be evidence, require certain postal/telecom data, and obtain bank-secrecy information in a criminal investigation under the Banking Law. In practical terms, that is the legal machinery for the sort of steps you mentioned: bank records, questioning counterparties and suppliers, and obtaining documents from third parties. citeturn41view0turn26view1turn26view3turn25view0

So, if your complaint is coherent and supported with the basics — contract/order trail, payment proof, correspondence, identity details, and a short chronology — **you are not expected to privately finance an evidentiary chase**. Your job is to report, document, and react to official communications. The coercive collection and verification powers belong to the State. citeturn20view0turn20view2turn41view0turn26view1turn25view0

## Your role as the victim

As a **pokrzywdzony**, you are not a passive bystander in the preparatory stage. Under **art. 299 § 1 KPK**, the victim is a **party** to the preparatory proceedings. Before the first interview, the victim is given written information about basic rights, including the ability to request certain acts such as questioning witnesses, to seek a legal representative including a court-appointed one, to access the file, and to challenge a discontinuation decision. You and your representative may also file motions to carry out investigative acts under **art. 315 § 1 KPK**. citeturn28view0turn34view0turn22view0

That means your practical role is this: you can **feed the file**, but you do not have to **run** the file. You can submit documents, propose witnesses, ask for specific evidence-gathering steps, and monitor what the prosecutor/Police do with the matter. In a fraud case, that often means targeted motions such as asking the authority to obtain bank-transfer data, hear named witnesses, secure business records, or verify whether the purported supplier chain was real. Those are sensible uses of your victim status; they do not convert you into the prosecutor. citeturn28view0turn22view0turn20view0turn26view1

If the authority refuses to open the case or later discontinues it, you have the **art. 306 KPK** appeal route. If the court quashes that refusal/discontinuance, its directions as to what still needs clarification or what acts must be performed are **binding** on the authority. If, after that, the authority again says there is no basis to indict, that second decision is challenged to the **superior prosecutor**; if it is upheld, the victim who has used the appeal path at least twice obtains the gateway to the subsidiary indictment under **art. 55 § 1 KPK**. citeturn27view2turn27view1

Once the public prosecutor files an indictment, you can become an **oskarżyciel posiłkowy** under **arts. 53–54 KPK** by making a declaration **until the opening of the judicial examination at the main hearing**. What that gives you is real standing in court: you become a **party alongside the public prosecutor**, you can make evidence motions because evidence is taken on motions of the parties or ex officio, you can question witnesses at trial, and as a party you have the right to appeal a first-instance judgment. It also gives you an important safety net: if the public prosecutor withdraws the indictment, that does **not** automatically strip you of your auxiliary-prosecutor rights. citeturn39view0turn43view0turn43view1turn43view2

For you as a non-Polish speaker, there is an additional benefit once you become an auxiliary prosecutor: **art. 56a KPK** requires that an appealable judgment or another final decision be served on an auxiliary prosecutor who does not sufficiently know Polish **together with a translation**. That is stronger protection than relying solely on informal explanation. citeturn39view0

## Lawyer, legal aid, and when counsel becomes mandatory

A private **pełnomocnik** is **not mandatory** for the ordinary victim steps you are worried about. Under **art. 87 § 1 KPK**, a party other than the accused **may** appoint a representative; the language is permissive, not compulsory. So you can file the complaint yourself, correspond with the prosecutor yourself, submit evidence motions yourself, file the first **art. 306** appeal yourself, and make the declaration that you will act as **oskarżyciel posiłkowy** yourself. citeturn29view1turn29view2turn39view0turn27view2

The point where the Code changes from “may” to “must” is the **subsidiary indictment**. Under **art. 55 § 2 KPK**, an indictment filed by the victim after the repeated refusal/umorzenie pathway must be **drawn up and signed by an advocate, legal adviser, or counsel from the State Treasury Solicitor’s Office**. If you ever reach that stage, you either need privately retained counsel or you need to obtain a **court-appointed pełnomocnik z urzędu** quickly enough for that lawyer to prepare it within the one-month statutory deadline from service of the superior prosecutor’s decision. citeturn39view0turn27view1

Yes, a victim can obtain a **court-appointed representative**. **Art. 88 KPK** makes the rules on appointed defense counsel apply correspondingly to a victim’s representative, and **art. 78 § 1 KPK** sets the means test: you must properly show that you cannot bear the costs without prejudice to the necessary maintenance of yourself and your family. Because **art. 78 § 1a** is also picked up through **art. 88**, appointment can be sought even **for a specific procedural act**. The appointing authority is the **president of the court or a judicial clerk of the court competent to hear the case**, including in the preparatory stage where the statute so provides. Once the representative is appointed, the remuneration is handled under the State-funded legal-aid regulations rather than by a private retainer between you and the lawyer. citeturn10view2turn10view3turn30view1turn38search0

Your being Swedish and living in Warsaw does not create a statutory nationality bar here. The Code’s appointed-counsel rules turn on **party status and inability to pay**, not on Polish citizenship. More broadly, Poland’s official victim-rights information states that non-EU victims’ rights are protected the same way as EU victims’ rights, save for a separate issue of certain State-compensation schemes. For you, the practical complication is evidentiary rather than legal: if your income, assets, rent burden, or obligations are documented abroad, you should be prepared to prove them clearly, ideally with readable attachments and, if necessary, translated summaries. citeturn34view0turn10view2turn10view3

There is also a useful no-cost alternative to a paid lawyer before you ever reach “mandatory counsel” territory. Poland’s **nieodpłatna pomoc prawna** and victim-support network are available to crime victims who cannot afford paid advice; the system works in roughly **1,500 points**, can be used **remotely**, and does not depend on residence in a particular county. It can explain your rights, help you understand the file, and prepare a **draft application** for court-appointed counsel. The important limitation is that it generally does **not** draft procedural pleadings for an already pending criminal investigation or court case. citeturn33view0turn33view1turn33view2

The sensible practical split, then, is this. **DIY is legally realistic** for: the initial complaint, factual supplements, document submissions, evidence motions, the first **art. 306** appeal, and the declaration to act as **oskarżyciel posiłkowy**. **Counsel is legally necessary** for: the subsidiary indictment. **Counsel is not legally necessary but can be strategically valuable** for: full trial advocacy in a contested fraud case, especially if there are multiple defendants, expert issues, or a dense document trail. That last point is a practical inference from the procedural structure, not a legal requirement. citeturn29view1turn39view0turn27view2turn43view0turn43view1turn43view2

## Attendance, language, and practical logistics

You will **often** be heard as a witness, but not literally in every case and not necessarily many times. The Code allows the authorities to omit questioning the victim as a witness if it is not needed for factual findings, but it also says that a victim who has not been questioned should be heard if he requests it, unless that would cause delay. If you are summoned as a witness, the general rule is that you must appear and testify. citeturn22view0turn15view0

On travel burden, the law gives you useful tools. **Art. 177 § 1a KPK** allows witness examination by videoconference with simultaneous audio-video transmission. That means you can ask the prosecutor or court to hear you **remotely** rather than drag you repeatedly to the venue court. Separately, in court proceedings, if the witness is in another district and attendance is hindered by obstacles that are too difficult to remove, the court may have the witness examined by a delegated judge or the local requested court. In plain English: **you can ask for remote or local handling**, but it is a matter of procedural decision, not an absolute entitlement on demand. citeturn15view0turn15view1

What you generally **cannot** do is replace witness testimony with a written narrative. The Code expressly says that evidence from witness testimony may not be substituted by letters, notes, or official memoranda. So a written chronology is excellent as a supporting annex and memory aid, but it does **not** normally replace an oral witness examination. citeturn15view0

For language, your rights are strong. Under **art. 204 KPK**, an interpreter must be called when there is a need to question a person who does not know Polish and also when a foreign-language document needs translating or a party must be acquainted with evidentiary content. Poland’s official victim-rights page on the European e-Justice Portal states that a translator assists in proceedings involving a non-Polish-speaking victim, that letters to or from the victim are translated, and that the victim does **not** bear the costs of those translations. Once you are an **oskarżyciel posiłkowy**, **art. 56a KPK** adds translation of appealable/final decisions served on you. citeturn13view0turn34view0turn39view0

## Realistic out-of-pocket cost

If you rely on the prosecutor and do **not** retain private counsel, your **mandatory** out-of-pocket can genuinely be very low. Filing the complaint costs **0 PLN**. Interpreter/translation support required for your participation as a non-Polish-speaking victim is borne by the State. If you are summoned as a **witness**, the Code gives you reimbursement for travel, lodging/maintenance, and lost earnings — but you must ask for it within **3 days** after the procedural act. That means hearing-related travel is often a temporary outlay rather than a final cost, so long as you claim it promptly. citeturn18search0turn34view0turn17view0turn17view1turn17view2

The ordinary places where money can still leave your pocket are the optional ones. If you order official copies from the file, the current fee regulation is **1 PLN per page** for ordinary copies and **6 PLN per page** for certified copies; copies on an electronic medium add **6 PLN** for the medium. And while the State can translate what it needs for the criminal proceedings, some victims choose to pay privately for a clean Polish translation of the key contract, invoice, payment confirmation, and selected correspondence so that the initial complaint is immediately digestible. citeturn32search1turn32search2turn32search10turn13view0turn34view0

For those voluntary translations, the official sworn-translator tariff is a useful benchmark even though private market rates may differ. In the 2025 consolidated regulation, the basic rate for translation **into Polish** is **44.07 PLN per 1,125 characters** from English, German, French, or Russian, and **53.07 PLN per 1,125 characters** from another European language such as Swedish, before uplifts for specialist terminology and other modifiers. That is why a small, focused bundle of key documents may cost only **hundreds of PLN**, while a large annex set can quickly move into the **low thousands** if you choose to pre-translate it yourself instead of waiting for the authority to commission what it needs. citeturn40search0turn40search1turn40search15

As a practical working estimate, a lean **DIY / prosecutor-led** strategy is often around **0 to 2,000 PLN out of pocket**. The low end assumes: you file the complaint yourself, use the State interpreter, request witness-expense reimbursement on time, and do not buy many file copies or private translations. The upper end usually reflects a voluntary, focused translation pack, some administrative copying, and perhaps a one-off private consultation for peace of mind. That estimate is an inference from the official fee components and reimbursement rules rather than a statutory cap. citeturn18search0turn17view0turn17view1turn17view2turn32search1turn40search15

If you hire your own lawyer throughout, cost becomes **contractual**, not fixed by statute. The official criminal fee schedules are only a floor/benchmark: as of the current 2026 text, the minimum fee schedule is **540 PLN** for a case covered by **dochodzenie** and **900 PLN** for a case covered by **śledztwo**; the Ministry of Justice noted those minimum criminal rates rose from 1 January 2026. Published law-firm prices illustrate that private-market rates are usually higher: consultations around **190–300 PLN**, drafting a complaint from about **600 PLN**, preparatory-stage representation from about **2,000 PLN**, and first-instance representation from about **2,000 PLN**. On that basis, a full private retainer in a contested fraud case of this size can realistically land in the **several-thousand-złoty** range and often around **5,000–15,000+ PLN**, depending on motions, hearings, travel, and whether the matter has to be pushed into trial or the subsidiary-indictment stage. That last range is a market estimate drawn from published examples, not a legal tariff. citeturn37search0turn37search1turn37search6turn35search0turn35search2turn35search8

For your stated goal — avoiding paying a lawyer to “run the case” where the State already does that — the legally accurate answer is: **file the notification yourself, use your victim rights actively, insist on interpreter support, and reserve scarce legal-spend for only the pressure points**: an appeal against refusal/discontinuance if needed, and above all the **subsidiary indictment** stage if the prosecution system fails you twice. citeturn41view0turn42view0turn27view2turn39view0turn34view0
