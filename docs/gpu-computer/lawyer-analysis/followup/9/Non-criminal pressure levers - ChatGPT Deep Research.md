# Non-criminal pressure levers against a sole proprietor with a clean KRZ

## Working conclusion

On the facts you gave, there **are** meaningful non-criminal pressure levers beyond an EPU claim and a criminal fraud complaint, but the strongest cheap ones do **not** all mature at the same time. The most important timing point is that, **based only on a debt due on 31 March 2026**, the Bankruptcy Law presumption of insolvency from a payment delay of more than three months has **not** yet clearly arisen as of 28 May 2026 or even 3 June 2026. On your dates alone, that means the cleanest statutory route to say “he missed the filing deadline” only becomes straightforward **around end-June 2026 for the presumption**, and **around end-July 2026 for the 30-day filing duty**. If, however, he had other due unpaid debts earlier, or you can prove broader inability to pay before then, the insolvency date can be earlier than the three-month presumption date. citeturn3view0turn50view0turn47view1

The “clean KRZ” point is still valuable, but mainly as evidence that his **email story about having already filed** is very weak. Debtor-filed bankruptcy petitions and the key dispositions on those petitions are publicly announced in the register, and KRZ is the public portal for insolvency-type proceedings. So if he told you he had already sought formal protection, the absence of any KRZ trace as of 28 May 2026 strongly undercuts that claim; what it does **not** prove, by itself, is that he was already in statutory default of the duty to file by that date. citeturn51view2turn44search4turn42view0

That leads to a practical ranking. If you want **pressure per złoty spent**, the best immediate lever is usually a **truthful, documented KAS notice** focused on VAT/accounting verification, because it is near-zero-cost and can trigger audit pressure without requiring you to prove insolvency timing. The next-best lever is an **article 373 business-ban application**, but only **once you can credibly show the bankruptcy filing deadline was missed** or you have evidence of earlier insolvency. A **creditor-filed bankruptcy petition** is real leverage, but it is no longer a low-cost lever: the filing fee is 1,000 PLN and the advance is tied to one average monthly enterprise wage, plus the court can demand more. It is therefore the most serious escalation and the one most likely to backfire economically if he is asset-poor. citeturn8view2turn6view1turn10view0turn45view3turn47view0

## Insolvency filing duty and the business-ban route

### Has the filing window likely expired

For a sole proprietor, article 21 of the Bankruptcy Law applies directly: the **debtor himself** must file within 30 days from the date the basis for bankruptcy arose. Article 11 makes insolvency exist when the debtor has lost the ability to pay due monetary obligations, and article 11 § 1a creates a presumption of that loss of ability when the delay exceeds three months. On your facts alone, that means you **cannot yet safely say** that the statutory filing window had already expired on 28 May 2026, because the debt due date you supplied was 31 March 2026. The clean insolvency/bankruptcy-failure narrative becomes much stronger only once the three-month presumption is live, unless you can show the debtor had already stopped paying other due debts earlier. citeturn3view0turn50view0

That distinction matters because a lot of creditors conflate “he has been stringing me along for months” with “the Bankruptcy Law deadline has already been missed.” In ordinary language, yes, a debtor can be delaying for months; in the statutory sense, article 373 pressure based purely on **failure to file in time** is strongest only after you can anchor the insolvency date. Your debtor’s alleged statement that he had already filed may still be powerful evidence of his awareness of distress, but timing under articles 11 and 21 still has to be proved or at least strongly inferred from objective facts. citeturn3view0turn50view0

### Civil liability under article 21 and what it is worth in a JDG case

Article 21 § 3 does give a creditor a damages route: persons obliged to file on time are liable for damage caused by not filing on time, unless they lack fault, and article 21 § 3a creates a presumption that, for a creditor of an insolvent debtor, the damage includes the amount of the unsatisfied claim. The debtor can try to escape that liability by proving lack of fault or, among other things, that a restructuring proceeding was timely opened or an arrangement was approved in time. citeturn50view0

In **your specific JDG scenario**, though, this is usually a **supplementary merits theory**, not a separate “extra pocket.” A sole proprietor already answers for the contractual debt with all present and future assets, because the debtor and the business are the same person. So article 21 § 3 is not as practically transformative as it is against company managers. My assessment is that it can still be pleaded or reserved, especially if you later need to explain asset depletion caused by the late filing, but it is **not** one of your best cheap pressure levers by itself. That is an inference from how article 21 works when the obliged person and the contract debtor are the same individual. citeturn50view0

### The article 373 business-ban application

Article 373 is a much sharper pressure tool. The court may impose, for **one to ten years**, a ban on conducting business on one’s own account or through a civil-law partnership and on acting as a succession manager, supervisory-board member, audit-committee member, representative, or proxy for a sole proprietor, commercial company, state enterprise, cooperative, foundation, or association, if the person, through his own fault, failed to file for bankruptcy on time when legally obliged to do so. The court weighs fault and the consequences of the conduct, including the extent of harm to creditors. A final order imposing the ban is publicly announced. That combination makes the measure professionally damaging in a way a simple money judgment often is not. citeturn50view1turn50view2

Yes — **you as a creditor may apply directly** for that ban, and you may do so independently of both the criminal track and the civil debt-collection track. The proceeding is started **only on application**, and article 376 expressly lists the creditor among the entitled applicants. It is a **non-contentious** proceeding, the court decides after a hearing, and even satisfaction of the applicant-creditor’s claim does **not** stop the case from continuing once started. In other words, if you file an article 373 application and he later pays only to make you go away, the public-interest proceeding does not automatically vanish. citeturn50view2

The cost profile is favorable. Under the court-costs statute, the fee for an application in a business-ban case is **100 PLN**. KRZ documents list a dedicated initiating form for “Wniosek o orzeczenie zakazu prowadzenia działalności gospodarczej” as form **20158**, and KRZ is the filing environment for these matters. For a Kraków entrepreneur, the insolvency division with territorial competence for the Kraków court area is the **VIII Commercial Division for bankruptcy and restructuring matters** at the District Court for Kraków-Śródmieście in Kraków. citeturn8view2turn43search11turn11search8turn44search0

The practical warning is timing. If you file the article 373 application **now**, and your only hard insolvency timestamp is the 31 March 2026 maturity date of your refund claim, the application risks being seen as premature because the missed-filing theory is not yet cleanly established. The better article 373 package is one filed either **after the deadline is clearly missed** on the chronology, or **earlier but with additional evidence** of prior insolvency: other overdue debts, bounced salary/tax obligations, supplier non-payment, failed execution, bank-account freezes, or admissions showing he had already stopped paying a wider group of creditors. The limitation rule is not your immediate issue: where no bankruptcy case was opened, the statute gives a longer time window to initiate the ban proceeding. citeturn50view2turn47view1

If you use this lever, the evidence bundle should be tightly built around the statutory elements: proof of your due and unpaid claim; the written refund promise; payment confirmation; your demands and his delay; the email where he said he had filed for bankruptcy protection; a KRZ print or screenshot showing no such proceeding; and any evidence that non-payment was broader than your transaction alone. On the present facts, I would score article 373 as **high pressure, very low cash cost, but timing-sensitive**. citeturn50view1turn50view2turn51view2

## Creditor-filed bankruptcy as pressure

### Standing, cost, and what the statute actually allows

A creditor can indeed force the issue procedurally. Article 20 says that a bankruptcy petition may be filed by the debtor or by **any personal creditor**. In other words, for standing purposes there is no special statutory minimum amount built into article 20; what matters is that you are a real personal creditor and that the debtor is insolvent. Your 155,000 PLN refund claim, especially with a written refund promise, comfortably clears the practical seriousness threshold even though the statute itself speaks in terms of creditor status rather than a minimum sum. citeturn51view1

The cost, however, is no longer “cheap pressure.” The fixed court fee for a bankruptcy petition is **1,000 PLN**. On top of that, article 22a requires an advance equal to **one average monthly wage in the enterprise sector without profit bonuses in the third quarter of the previous year**. The GUS notice for Q3 2025 gives that figure as **8,851.42 PLN**, and the court may also demand an additional advance above the article 22a amount under article 32 § 5. So the realistic cash exposure is: **1,000 PLN fee + 8,851.42 PLN advance + possible extra advance**. citeturn8view2turn6view1turn10view0turn45view3

There are also two important doctrinal constraints. First, article 12a says the court will dismiss a creditor’s petition if the debtor proves the claim is **entirely disputed** and that the dispute existed before the petition was filed. Second, article 13 says the court will dismiss the petition if the debtor’s estate is insufficient even to cover the costs of the proceedings, or only enough to cover those costs, unless there is a credible path to recover value through ineffectiveness or avoidance theories. That is the heart of the “money-sink” risk. citeturn47view0turn47view2

### When it creates real leverage and when it backfires

As a pressure device, a creditor bankruptcy petition is serious because it can take on a life of its own. Article 29a says the court may treat withdrawal as inadmissible if withdrawal would prejudice creditors, and payment to the petitioning creditor after filing does **not** affect the further course of the bankruptcy-opening proceeding. So if your goal is “pay me and I will quietly go away,” a bankruptcy petition is a lever that can become harder to control than a business-ban motion. citeturn45view0turn45view1

There are some creditor-friendly cost rules on paper. If you withdraw after the debtor performs the obligations described in article 11, court costs are charged to the debtor; and if the petition is dismissed under article 13, the debtor bears the court costs and must also reimburse the costs you incurred. But where a debtor is truly asset-poor, that formal cost allocation may be economically hollow. It gives you a costs claim against someone who may still be unable to pay. citeturn45view2turn45view3

There is also a creditor-risk side. If the court concludes the petition was filed in **bad faith**, article 34 authorizes costs against the creditor, a possible order to publish an appropriate public statement, and a damages claim by the debtor or even a third party. With your documentary case, that bad-faith risk does **not** look high, but it is a reason to keep the petition factual and insolvency-focused rather than rhetorical. citeturn46view1turn46view2

My practical assessment is this: a creditor bankruptcy petition makes sense when you have at least one of four things — a believable asset trail, evidence of receivables or inventory, plausible avoidance targets, or a strategic reason to create maximum institutional pressure even at real cost. It makes **less** sense when the likely outcome is either article 13 dismissal for no estate or a thin collective proceeding in which your bilateral leverage disappears. For your file, absent signs of assets or clawback targets, I would treat this as a **heavy escalation**, not a default low-cost move. citeturn47view0turn45view1turn46view1

## KAS and VAT pressure

### What the VAT theory depends on

This lever is potentially excellent, but only if you frame it correctly. Under the VAT Act, if a seller receives all or part of payment before delivery or service, the VAT tax point generally arises **upon receipt of that amount**, and the invoice for that advance should generally be issued **by the 15th day of the following month**. So if GPUcomputer was a VAT-taxable seller on that 155,000 PLN prepayment, then the advance itself is the key VAT event, not the later non-delivery. citeturn19view3turn17view1

At the same time, you should **not** assume VAT was definitely due. A seller whose sales are VAT-exempt under article 113 is generally not obliged to issue an invoice for that exempt sale unless the buyer asks, and article 113 currently sets the domestic exemption threshold at **240,000 PLN** of sales value. Also, a **pro forma is not a VAT invoice**; official government materials explicitly say that “pro forma” is not a factura in the VAT sense. So the correct tax complaint is not “he definitely evaded VAT,” but rather: **please verify his VAT status, whether the advance was taxable, whether a proper invoice or other documentation was issued, and whether the receipt of funds was properly declared.** citeturn17view1turn17view3turn49search0turn49search12

### Which KKS provisions are the realistic fit

On the KKS side, the best fit is usually article 56 if a taxpayer filed a declaration or statement containing falsehoods or omissions that exposed tax to underpayment, and article 62 if someone, contrary to duty, failed to issue an invoice or issued it defectively. Article 76, by contrast, is targeted at causing a **groundless tax refund, overpayment refund, or similar fiscal return**, so it is **not** the cleanest fit for your present fact pattern unless the file later shows false input-VAT refunds or similar refund behavior. citeturn37search3turn38search9turn38search1

That means the KAS notice should be framed around **verification**, not overcharging. A good factual formulation would be:  
the business took a 155,000 PLN prepayment for undelivered goods; the seller later failed to refund despite a written refund promise; the seller reportedly claimed formal insolvency protection but nothing appears in KRZ; please verify VAT status, invoice/documentation, declaration of the advance, and whether business activity is being run through other persons or outside the declared fiscal profile. That is a strong near-zero-cost pressure lever because an audit risk often matters to a small operator even when a civil judgment does not. citeturn19view3turn17view1turn51view2

### Standing, procedure, and why this is mostly an audit-pressure lever

You are correct that in a fiscal-penal matter you do **not** have the normal “pokrzywdzony” role the way you would in an ordinary property-crime case. The KKS says KPK provisions concerning the injured party do not apply here, and the listed parties in fiscal-criminal proceedings are the public prosecutor, the accused, the entity drawn into subsidiary liability, and the intervenor — not the private victim-creditor. In practice, that means you are an **informant and witness**, not the rights-bearing injured party in the KKS case. citeturn40view0turn40view1

That does **not** reduce the lever’s practical value. For preparatory proceedings, article 133 allocates major KKS cases such as article 56 § 1 and article 76 § 1 to the **naczelnik urzędu celno-skarbowego**, while the naczelnik urzędu skarbowego handles the remaining fiscal offenses and misdemeanors. The cheapest filing routes are official KAS channels: the nationwide **KTI KAS** phone line and electronic reporting form, or a written submission through **e-Urząd Skarbowy** / an electronic-delivery address / paper filing. Since 1 January 2026, an ePUAP submission by a non-public sender to tax or KAS authorities is generally **not** a legally effective route for this kind of podanie. citeturn41view1turn16view0turn16view1turn16view2turn15view0

In practical terms, the notice should attach only objective items: proof of payment, the pro forma, the refund promise, the due date, the non-delivery chronology, his email about “bankruptcy protection,” and the KRZ result. Do **not** speculate about offenses you cannot ground. The leverage comes from triggering a compliance look, not from your ability to act as a party. If you want the highest signal-to-noise ratio, route the matter either through KTI KAS or as a written notice asking for tax verification rather than using dramatic penal labels. citeturn16view0turn16view1turn16view2

### What Waldek’s non-VAT-registration does and does not prove

By itself, the fact that Waldek is “not VAT-registered” does **not** prove a tax irregularity. Some taxpayers are legitimately exempt from VAT registration because of the domestic exemption threshold or because of the nature of their activities. So you should not present that fact alone as a smoking gun. citeturn17view3turn18view2

What **is** worth flagging is a different point: if Waldek is the person who in reality runs the commercial side, handles funds, issues or withholds invoices, or otherwise manages the enterprise’s economic affairs, that matters both evidentially and legally. The KKS extends liability not only to the nominal actor but also to a person who, on the basis of law, contract, decision, or **factual performance**, handles the economic affairs of another entity. So his role may be relevant not because he is unregistered for VAT, but because he may be a **de facto operator** of the business and therefore a person KAS should look at. citeturn26view0

## Consumer and UOKiK angle

As a **supplementary** lever, UOKiK is usually weak for a one-off dispute like this unless you can credibly show a broader pattern affecting consumers. UOKiK’s own guidance says that **anyone may notify** it of a practice infringing the **collective** interests of consumers, but for **individual** matters one should use consumer-help channels instead. If your purchase was genuinely for personal use, the more practical consumer institution is often the local or municipal **consumer ombudsman** or the Trade Inspection’s ADR mechanisms, not UOKiK itself. citeturn48search1turn48search2turn48search5

So my short answer is: **worth only as a modest add-on** if you truly have a consumer fact pattern and especially if you have signs he is doing the same thing to others. It is **not** the best primary pressure lever for a single transaction that looks outwardly business-facing. UOKiK can start administrative proceedings on its own initiative, but the expected payoff from a lone notice in a one-transaction case is usually much lower than with KAS or a ripe article 373 application. citeturn48search1turn48search5

## Best bang-for-buck ranking and backfire warnings

**First place: a fact-heavy KAS notice.**  
This is the best immediate ratio of pressure to cost. It costs almost nothing, does not require you to prove the bankruptcy-filing deadline has already been missed, and can create real audit pressure. The key warning is not to overstate the VAT theory: because a pro forma is not a VAT invoice and a seller may be VAT-exempt, your notice should ask KAS to **verify** VAT status, advance-payment treatment, and documentation rather than accusing him of definite VAT fraud. citeturn19view3turn17view1turn17view3turn49search12turn40view0

**Second place: an article 373 business-ban application, but only once ripe.**  
This is the strongest cheap reputational/professional-pressure tool because the fee is only 100 PLN, a creditor has direct standing, the proceeding continues even if your own claim is later paid, the sanction can last one to ten years, and the final order is publicly announced. The backfire risk is mainly **prematurity**: on your present dates, if your only hard insolvency marker is the 31 March 2026 maturity date, filing article 373 **now** would be too early unless you have proof of earlier general non-payment. citeturn50view1turn50view2turn8view2

**Third place: article 21 § 3 as a supplemental civil theory, not as a stand-alone pressure move.**  
You can use it in principle, and the statute even presumes damage to include the amount of the unpaid claim. But for a JDG debtor it usually adds less tactical value than it does against company managers, because you are still pointing at the same individual pocket. I would treat it as a reinforcing legal basis for ordinary litigation, not as one of your top separate pressure devices. citeturn50view0

**Fourth place: a creditor-filed bankruptcy petition.**  
This is credible escalation, but not cheap. The fee is 1,000 PLN; the statutory advance is 8,851.42 PLN on current official figures; and the court may demand more. It becomes rational when you have evidence of assets, receivables, inventory, or avoidance targets, or when you are intentionally escalating from bilateral pressure to formal insolvency pressure. It can backfire by consuming cash, by ending in article 13 dismissal if the estate is empty, or by shifting the matter into collective insolvency logic where your bilateral collection leverage shrinks. Also, once you file, later payment to you does not necessarily let you cleanly shut the process down. citeturn8view2turn6view1turn10view0turn47view0turn45view1turn46view1

**Fifth place: UOKiK / consumer machinery.**  
Use this only if you can **honestly** frame the matter as consumer-facing and preferably show it is not an isolated event. For a single transaction that presents as business-like, it is mostly a weak supplement. The better consumer-side institution in a truly personal-use case is usually the consumer ombudsman, not UOKiK. citeturn48search1turn48search2turn48search5

The sharpest practical sequencing, on your facts, is therefore this: **prepare the KAS packet now; preserve the article 373 packet now but do not fire it prematurely; use a creditor bankruptcy petition only if you uncover assets or want very hard escalation despite cost; and treat UOKiK as optional background noise rather than a main tool.** If you wanted one sentence that captures the whole file, it would be: **KAS now, article 373 later when ripe, bankruptcy only with asset logic, UOKiK only as garnish.** citeturn16view0turn50view2turn47view0turn48search1
