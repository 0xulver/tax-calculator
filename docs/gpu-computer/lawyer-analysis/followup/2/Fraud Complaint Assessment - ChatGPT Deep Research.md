## Decision tree

Poland does **not** publish a clean public statistic for your exact slice of case: a single known victim, sub-200k advance payment, identified sole-trader seller, and suspect living abroad. ## What moves the odds

In this category of case, the battlefield is **not** “did he fail to deliver?” The battlefield is: **what did he know, and what did he hide, when he took the 155,000 PLN?** Polish case law cuts hard on that line. One appellate judgment states plainly that intent to obtain advantage by not honoring the undertaking must be proven and cannot simply be presumed; a Supreme Court judgment also accepts fraud where the counterparty consciously concealed an objectively existing situation affecting the ability to perform the contract. citeturn24search1turn24search14

**The highest-impact item is the bank trail showing there was never a real procurement path.** If the 155k was not used to pay the supplier, reserve components, pay customs, or otherwise build the machine, but instead was transferred out, consumed, used to plug old holes, or mingled with a collapsing cash position, that is your cleanest bridge from “broken promise” to “deceptive taking of money.” Prosecutors struggle with pure words; they react far better to bank statements that kill the commercial-performance story. citeturn24search1turn24search14

**Very close behind is surfacing other victims with the same modus operandi.** For court proof on your count, the bank trail may be the single strongest item. But for getting the prosecutor to stop treating the affair as a one-off business failure, other victims are transformative. A pattern of similar intake of money, similar excuses, and similar non-delivery changes the file from “possible civil dispute” to “possible scheme.” It also matters later because a refusal or discontinuance can be taken up again when new important facts or evidence appear. citeturn24search1turn34view1

**Proof that the Hong Kong supplier story was fake, impossible, or unsupported is also very powerful.** If there was no real supplier, no invoice chain, no customs clearance, no shipping documents, no purchase order, and no genuine bankruptcy event matching the timeline, then the contradictory explanations stop looking like ordinary scrambling and start looking like post-payment fabrication. That goes directly to concealment of the true position and helps satisfy the fraud line recognized in the case law. citeturn24search14turn24search4

**Contradictory written statements and the 10 March refund promise matter, but less than people think.** They are good evidence that the story shifted and that liability was acknowledged. But they are mostly **corroborative**, not decisive. A prosecutor can still say: “This proves debt and post-default lying, not necessarily fraudulent intent at the moment of payment.” In other words, the refund promise helps you, but it is not the killer exhibit unless it is coupled with the bank trail or fake-supplier proof. citeturn24search1turn24search14

**Evidence about Waldek matters mainly for widening the suspect pool and leverage, not automatically for cash.** If you can prove that he knowingly operated the mailbox, made the refund promise, and participated in the false story, that is one genuine advantage of the criminal route over a simple EPU claim. But by itself it is more useful for building co-perpetration or aiding allegations than for proving that cash is collectible. citeturn24search1turn34view0

## How money can come back inside the criminal case

Stripped of legal decoration, there are only **three** realistic pathways to money inside the criminal track: **repayment under pressure**, **an Article 46 compensation order**, or **asset security that later feeds execution**. A conviction by itself is **not** money. It is, at best, a stronger platform for trying to get money. citeturn55view0turn53view0turn54view0

### Article 46 compensation order

Article 46 § 1 KK says that **upon conviction** the court **may** order repair of damage or compensation, but **on the victim’s motion it must do so**. That is the crucial distinction. Do **not** assume the court will handle this for you automatically. If you want Article 46 relief, file the motion. Article 49a KPK lets the victim or prosecutor file that motion **until the close of the main-trial evidentiary phase**. In practice, the safe move is to file it as soon as the case reaches court and then repeat or maintain it at the first hearing. citeturn55view0turn52view0

So the honest answers to your sub-questions are these. **When can you get it?** Only if there is a **conviction**. **Is it automatic?** No. The court has ex officio discretion, but if you file the motion in time, the statute switches to a mandatory formulation. **Do you need a separate civil merits judgment?** No. You do **not** need to sue again to establish liability for the same criminally caused damage. The criminal judgment is the merits basis for enforcement; if money or other assets were already secured in the criminal case, the execution rules in the criminal enforcement code allow the final obligation to be satisfied out of that secured property. The code even says that where the beneficiary did not participate in the case, the court sends the execution title ex officio and without fees. The practical point is simple: Article 46 can spare you a second merits lawsuit, but it still does **not** manufacture solvency. citeturn55view0turn26view0turn26view1turn26view2turn26view3

Another useful point: Article 46 § 3 KK expressly says that awarding damages or compensation in the criminal judgment does **not** block pursuit of any **unsatisfied remainder** in civil proceedings. So if the criminal court awards less than the full amount or collection later falls short, the civil route remains relevant for the gap. citeturn55view0

### Asset security under KPK

For your case, **zabezpieczenie majątkowe** is the most important criminal mechanism that can lead to actual money rather than just moral satisfaction. But it is not available on day one merely because you say you were cheated. Under Article 291 § 1 KPK, this security arises, for your kind of recovery interest, **once a specific person has been formally accused/suspected in a way that activates the criminal case against him**, and there is a justified fear that without security execution would be impossible or much harder. There are broader pre-charge exceptions in Article 291 § 2, but those mainly concern **forfeiture / proceeds of crime**, not your ordinary victim-compensation angle. For **your** 155k-recovery purpose, the practical answer is: **yes, effectively you usually need suspect/accused status first**. citeturn53view0

What can be frozen? Quite a lot. Article 292 KPK sends the mode of security to civil-execution mechanisms, and the statute specifically mentions **movables, receivables and other property rights**, plus a **ban on selling or encumbering real estate**, recorded in the land register. Article 292a adds a heavier weapon: **forced management of an enterprise** by an appointed administrator. Article 295 lets the police do a **temporary seizure of movable property** of a suspect if there is a risk it will be removed, but that temporary seizure falls away if a formal security order is not issued within 7 days. citeturn53view0turn54view0

Who decides? In preparatory proceedings, **the prosecutor** issues the security order; after indictment, **the court** does. For forced management of an enterprise, a prosecutor’s order must be submitted to the court for approval within 7 days, and the court decides within 7 days after receiving it. The order is appealable, but Article 293 § 5 KPK is the point you care about: the order becomes an **enforceable execution basis immediately upon issuance**. That makes criminal security potentially much sharper than people expect. citeturn54view0

What does it cost you? Usually, the answer is not money but **information**. The measure is structured as an **ex officio** state tool. Your real job is to hand the prosecutor usable asset intelligence: property, receivables, bank relationships, vehicles, business assets, counterparties. Without that, the formal existence of Article 291 does not help much. With that, it can matter a lot. Official 2024 prosecution statistics reported **48,138** cases in which asset security existed in court-bound or similar concluded matters, with an estimated secured value of about **PLN 1.198 billion**. That does **not** mean your case will get a freeze; it does show that the mechanism is real, used, and worth pursuing where assets can actually be identified. citeturn53view0turn54view0turn8view0

Compared with the civil-security route your lawyer says is not realistically available, the cleanest way to say it is this: **criminal security arrives later, but bites harder**. It is **not** a substitute for easy front-end civil security before you have crossed the criminal threshold. But once the file is treated as a genuine fraud case against a concrete suspect, the criminal toolset becomes materially more coercive. citeturn53view0turn54view0

### Repayment pressure, mediation, and negotiated endings

Formal criminal mediation exists, but you should not build your strategy around it. In 2024 the prosecution service reported only **397** referrals to mediation in preparatory proceedings, and **286** settlements. That is tiny in the context of overall criminal throughput. The practical reason repayment still matters is different: the sentencing rules treat **repair of damage**, **reconciliation**, and **positive mediation results** as mitigating circumstances, and both negotiated consensus routes under Articles **335/343 KPK** and **387 KPK** require that the victim, properly notified, does **not object**. citeturn8view0turn51view0turn49view0turn50view1

That creates real leverage. Article 53 KK tells the court to consider the offender’s efforts to repair the damage, lists reconciliation and compensation as mitigating factors, and separately tells the court to consider positive mediation results. Article 343 KPK allows the court to condition a fast-track agreed conviction on repair of damage where Article 46 is not being used, and Article 387 KPK likewise permits a negotiated conviction without full evidentiary trial only if the prosecutor agrees and the duly informed victim does not object. In plain English: **repayment can buy the suspect a better criminal outcome**, and your non-objection can matter. That is why defendants in this type of file sometimes pay not because they became moral, but because they became rational. citeturn51view0turn49view0turn50view1

So when does pressure realistically peak? Usually not at the moment of filing. Usually it peaks **after the first serious investigative steps**—bank subpoenas, customs/document requests, witness interviews, or a first formal suspect event—and again when the defense starts thinking about a quick consensual ending. If repayment is going to happen, it is more likely in that window than after years of fully litigated trial and appeal. citeturn8view0turn34view0turn51view0turn50view1

## Honest verdict on whether this gets your money back

My honest verdict is: **conditional, and the condition is assets plus proof of original deceit**. **Yes**, this route can return the money where the debtor is still solvent, still exposed to pressure, and still has reachable assets, so that the fear of charges, a freeze, or a negotiated criminal outcome pushes a settlement; it can also work where Article 46 is awarded and there is preserved property to execute against. **No**, this route often returns **nothing in cash** where the money has already been dissipated and there are no attachable assets, because a conviction then gives you mainly a stronger piece of paper and more leverage, not actual funds. In my cold expected-value view, the criminal route has perhaps a **15–30%** chance of putting **most** of the 155k back into your hands, mostly through settlement or preserved assets, and a **better-than-even** chance of producing **zero cash** while still delivering strategic value by increasing pressure, widening the suspect pool, and improving your evidence position for later fights. So the right answer is neither “yes” nor “no.” It is: **money back is possible, but only if the file reaches coercive traction before the assets are gone**. citeturn8view0turn55view0turn53view0turn54view0turn51view0

## Cost-benefit if you self-run with light counsel

On the assumptions you gave me—free filing, prosecutor-funded investigation, minimal paid counsel—I would call the criminal complaint **modestly positive expected value**, but only in the lean version. The reasons are these: in preparatory proceedings the victim is a **party**, so you are not a spectator; you can appeal a refusal to open and a discontinuance; and if later new evidence surfaces, a discontinued or refused case can be taken up again. Those are real rights. But the “modestly” matters. If you start paying for full-service lawyer monitoring for 2–3 years, or if you end up having to fund a *subsydiarny akt oskarżenia*, the economics can worsen quickly. The statute itself tells you where the expensive cliff is: a subsidiary indictment must be drafted and signed by an advocate or legal counsel. So my practical answer is: **yes, the cheap version is worth doing because the downside cash cost is low and the leverage upside is real; no, the expensive version is not a rational primary collection strategy unless new victims, bank-trail proof, or assets emerge.** citeturn54view0turn35view0turn35view1turn35view2turn34view1
