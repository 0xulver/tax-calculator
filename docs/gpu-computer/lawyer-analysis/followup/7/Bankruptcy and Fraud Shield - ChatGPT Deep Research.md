# Bankruptcy Shield for a PLN 155,000 Fraud Claim Under Polish Consumer Bankruptcy

## Statutory framework

Under Polish consumer-bankruptcy law, the discharge mechanism is not absolute. The core discharge rule is in **art. 491^21 ust. 1 PU**: after the debtor performs the repayment plan, the court issues an order confirming completion of the plan and **discharging pre-bankruptcy debts left unpaid**. But the statute immediately carves out a closed list of debts that **do not** get discharged. During the repayment-plan period, enforcement is generally stayed, **except** for claims falling within that protected list under art. 491^21 ust. 2. The same carve-out applies when the court grants discharge **without** a repayment plan, including conditional discharge under art. 491^16. citeturn1view0turn27view1turn28view0

The current text of **art. 491^21 ust. 2 PU** is the key provision. In Polish, it provides:

> „Nie podlegają umorzeniu zobowiązania o charakterze alimentacyjnym, zobowiązania wynikające z rent z tytułu odszkodowania za wywołanie choroby, niezdolności do pracy, kalectwa lub śmierci, zobowiązania do zapłaty orzeczonych przez sąd kar grzywny, a także do wykonania obowiązku naprawienia szkody oraz zadośćuczynienia za doznaną krzywdę, zobowiązania do zapłaty nawiązki lub świadczenia pieniężnego orzeczonych przez sąd jako środek karny lub środek związany z poddaniem sprawcy próbie, jak również zobowiązania do naprawienia szkody wynikającej z przestępstwa lub wykroczenia stwierdzonego prawomocnym orzeczeniem oraz zobowiązania, których upadły umyślnie nie ujawnił, jeżeli wierzyciel nie brał udziału w postępowaniu.” My English translation: support obligations; certain personal-injury/death annuities; criminal fines; obligations to perform a duty to repair damage and pay compensation for non-pecuniary harm; criminal or probationary monetary measures; obligations to repair damage resulting from a crime or petty offense established by a final judgment; and intentionally concealed debts where the creditor did not participate. citeturn1view0

Two features matter for your case. First, **the statute does not use a broad formula such as “all intentional tort debts are non-dischargeable.”** The current text is narrower and more technical. It protects, among other things, **court-ordered compensation obligations** and **damage claims resulting from a crime or petty offense established by a final judgment**. Second, this protected list applies not only after a normal repayment plan, but also in the “no-plan” discharge route under **art. 491^16 ust. 3 PU**, which expressly makes **art. 491^21 ust. 2 and 3** applicable by analogy. citeturn1view0turn28view0turn32view0

The practical consequence is explicit in two companion rules. While a repayment plan is being performed, **art. 491^15 ust. 6 PU** bars new enforcement of pre-plan claims **except** claims arising from obligations mentioned in **art. 491^21 ust. 2**. After the final discharge order, **art. 491^21 ust. 3 PU** again blocks enforcement of pre-plan claims **except** those same protected claims. So if your debt falls within art. 491^21 ust. 2, it is not merely “less vulnerable”; it sits outside the discharge mechanism itself. citeturn27view1turn1view0

A separate, useful fallback appears in the last limb of art. 491^21 ust. 2: debts the bankrupt **intentionally failed to disclose**, where the creditor did not participate in the case, also do not get discharged. So if a debtor ever omits your claim on purpose, that omission can itself preserve enforceability, even apart from the fraud/crime route. citeturn1view0

## Direct answer on art. 46 KK

**Yes — a final art. 46 KK compensation order, by itself, is the strongest and cleanest bankruptcy shield for this kind of claim.** A final criminal judgment ordering the debtor to repair the damage under **art. 46 § 1 KK** fits the first protected limb of **art. 491^21 ust. 2 PU**: an obligation “to perform the duty to repair damage” or to pay compensation for non-pecuniary harm. No separate civil judgment is required for that amount. citeturn44view0turn1view0turn24view0

That answer is reinforced by the present criminal-procedure architecture. **Art. 46 § 1 KK** allows the criminal court, on the victim’s motion, to order repair of the damage, applying civil-law rules. **Art. 107 § 2 KPK** states that judgments imposing a duty to repair damage or pay compensation for non-pecuniary harm are treated as judgments on property claims if they are capable of enforcement under the Code of Civil Procedure. The Supreme Court has repeatedly described the art. 46 measure as having a **compensatory, civil-law character**, not just a penal character, and as creating an executable title with res judicata effect for the same underlying compensation claim. citeturn44view0turn11view2turn10view0turn16view0

There is, however, one major caveat for your strategy: **art. 46 is not freely available if you are already litigating the same claim civilly.** Under **art. 415 § 1 KPK**, the criminal court does **not** order repair of damage or compensation if “the claim arising from the offense” is already the subject of another proceeding or has already been finally adjudicated. The Supreme Court has treated an art. 46 award as legally concerning the same compensation claim that could have been pursued in civil court, and once such a criminal compensation award exists, it blocks relitigation of the same claim civilly; the anti-cumulation rule works in the other direction too. So: **art. 46 alone can absolutely produce a bankruptcy-proof title, but only if the same 155,000 PLN claim is not already pending or finally awarded elsewhere.** citeturn11view0turn10view0

For your specific setup, that means the interaction with the EPU track is crucial. If your EPU is already pursuing the **same 155,000 PLN loss**, the criminal court may be procedurally barred from granting an art. 46 order for that same amount. In that scenario, the criminal route may still deliver something extremely valuable — a final finding of fraud under **art. 286 § 1 KK** — but the “criminal-only title” route under art. 46 can be weakened or closed by the pending civil case. citeturn11view0turn10view0turn44view1

## What actually triggers the shield

In your type of case, there are **two legally distinct ways** to make the 155,000 PLN bankruptcy-resistant under **art. 491^21 ust. 2 PU**.

The first route is the strongest: a **final court order imposing a duty to repair damage**, especially under **art. 46 § 1 KK**. Modern commentary reads the first protected limb of art. 491^21 ust. 2 as aimed precisely at obligations expressly imposed in criminal matters, especially under **art. 46 § 1 KK**, **art. 67 § 3 KK**, and **art. 72 § 2 KK**. On that reading, the statute is protecting the compensatory obligation itself, not requiring a separate civil title on top of it. citeturn24view0

The second route is more indirect: an obligation to repair damage **resulting from a crime or petty offense established by a final judgment**. The best reading in current commentary is that this second limb serves a different function from the first one. It covers the situation where the underlying conduct has been finally established in the criminal sphere, but the damages obligation is then pursued or quantified outside the criminal compensation order — for example in civil proceedings. That same commentary also takes the position that **only the criminal court really “establishes” a crime as such for this clause**, whereas a civil court can make factual findings about fraudulent conduct but does not itself “state that a crime was committed” in the statutory sense. citeturn25view0

That is why a **mere civil judgment** is not the safest way to think about this shield. I have not found a controlling authority saying that an ordinary civil judgment which merely describes intentional fraud-like conduct, **without** a final criminal adjudication or criminal compensation order, automatically falls within art. 491^21 ust. 2. The better-supported reading is narrower: the first limb is for **express compensation duties imposed in criminal proceedings**, and the second limb is for **damage claims whose criminal source has already been finally established in a criminal judgment**. In other words, a plain EPU title by itself is usually not the “nuclear option.” citeturn24view0turn25view0

For **art. 286 § 1 KK**, the underlying offense is inherently intentional. The statute defines fraud as causing another person to disadvantageously dispose of property **“w celu osiągnięcia korzyści majątkowej”** — with the aim of obtaining a financial benefit. That mens rea structure is exactly why an art. 286 conviction is such a strong bankruptcy-shield trigger: it gives you a final criminal establishment of an intentional property crime, and if the court adds an art. 46 compensation order, you get both protected limbs working in your favor at once. citeturn44view1turn44view0turn1view0

Timing matters a lot. Reported authority and commentary show that a creditor can still obtain a judgment **after** a repayment plan has been set and, while the plan is still running, seek to have the plan adjusted under **art. 491^19 ust. 4 PU** for claims that were finally confirmed only later. A regional court decision expressly notes that, **until the discharge order is actually issued**, it is still possible for a creditor omitted from the plan to obtain a final judgment and then use the bankruptcy mechanisms available during the plan period. citeturn30view0turn37view0

The harder question is whether a **later** criminal conviction, obtained only **after** the final discharge order under **art. 491^21 ust. 1 PU** or the no-plan route of **art. 491^16 PU**, can retroactively revive a debt that has already been discharged as ordinary debt. I did not find a clear authority endorsing that. The materials I found focus on later judgments **before** discharge, especially through **art. 491^19 ust. 4 PU**, and the Bankruptcy Law generally excludes ordinary civil-law reopening rules unless the Act says otherwise. The prudent, litigation-grade answer is therefore this: **if you want the shield, you want the criminal judgment or art. 46 order in place before the bankruptcy discharge order becomes final.** citeturn30view0turn37view0turn40view0

## Survival, enforceability, and limitation

If the shield applies, the debt **survives** consumer bankruptcy. That is true during the repayment-plan phase and after the final discharge order, because the enforcement bans in **art. 491^15 ust. 6 PU** and **art. 491^21 ust. 3 PU** both expressly exclude the debts listed in **art. 491^21 ust. 2**. In practical terms, that means the 155,000 PLN is not washed out by oddłużenie and remains collectible from later wages, bank funds, or assets acquired after bankruptcy, instead of becoming a worthless paper judgment. citeturn27view1turn1view0

An art. 46-based award is also a real enforcement title, not just a symbolic criminal add-on. Under **art. 107 § 1–2 KPK**, the court may grant enforceability to an enforceable criminal judgment, and judgments imposing repair of damage or compensation are treated as judgments on property claims. Under **art. 26 KKW**, the usual civil-enforcement provisions on executable titles apply, and **art. 196 § 1 KKW** says that where the beneficiary did not participate in the case, the court sends the enforceable title to the victim ex officio and without any fee. The Supreme Court has likewise emphasized that the art. 46 award is an executable title for the same compensation claim and has the same practical finality effects as a civil judgment. citeturn11view2turn8view1turn8view2turn10view0

As for limitation, the baseline civil rule is **art. 125 § 1 KC**: a claim confirmed by a final court judgment prescribes after **six years**, and future periodic payments prescribe after **three years**. Each enforcement or court step taken directly to pursue, secure, or satisfy the claim interrupts prescription under **art. 123 KC**, and after interruption the period runs anew under **art. 124 KC**; where interruption occurs through proceedings before a court or enforcement body, it does not restart until that proceeding ends. For a creditor, that means a protected claim can be kept alive for a very long time if you actively enforce it instead of letting the title go stale. citeturn12view0turn12view2turn12view3

There is some doctrinal noise because **art. 103 § 2 KK** applies, by analogy, the ten-year criminal-law execution limitation to compensatory measures. But the Supreme Court has underlined that, when what is being enforced is the victim’s civil compensation claim awarded in criminal proceedings, the enforceability question is governed by the **civil limitation rules in arts. 117–125 KC**, not by treating the claim as a criminal fine. The safe creditor practice is therefore simple: treat the claim as a civil judgment debt, obtain enforceability promptly, and interrupt limitation periodically through real enforcement activity. citeturn14view0turn16view0

## If he files in Poland or moves COMI to the Netherlands

If the main insolvency proceeding is opened **in Poland**, the Dutch side should respect the Polish discharge architecture. Under the EU Insolvency Regulation, the law of the Member State opening the proceedings governs the **opening, conduct, and closure** of the insolvency case, including **creditors’ rights after closure**. Judgments opening insolvency proceedings are automatically recognized across the EU, and the judgment opening main proceedings produces in every other Member State the same effects it has under the law of the state of opening. So if a Polish consumer-bankruptcy discharge does **not** extinguish an art. 491^21 ust. 2 debt, that non-dischargeability should travel with the Polish insolvency effect across the EU, including the Netherlands. citeturn42view1turn42view2turn42view3

The risk changes if the debtor first succeeds in shifting his **centre of main interests**. For an individual who is not carrying on an independent business or profession, the Insolvency Regulation presumes COMI at the place of the person’s **habitual residence**, unless rebutted, and that presumption does not apply if residence was moved within the six months before the request to open proceedings. If main proceedings are opened **in the Netherlands** rather than Poland, then Dutch insolvency law, not Polish art. 491^21 PU, governs the discharge effects as the lex concursus. In that scenario, a Polish title still matters enormously as a debt-establishing instrument, but the discharge/non-discharge question will be filtered through Dutch insolvency law rather than automatically through Polish non-dischargeability rules. citeturn42view1

That means the cross-border answer is asymmetric. **Polish proceedings:** the Netherlands should recognize Polish non-dischargeability effects. **Dutch proceedings after a real COMI shift:** Polish art. 491^21 stops being the governing discharge rule, so your protection will depend on how Dutch insolvency law treats that title and that category of debt. On present evidence, the safest anti-bankruptcy move remains obtaining a final criminal fraud adjudication and, ideally, an art. 46 compensation order **before** any foreign main proceeding is opened. citeturn42view1turn42view2turn42view3

## Strategic verdict

The short strategic answer is that **the shield is important enough to justify the criminal track** if the evidence of fraudulent intent at inception is at least decent and the debtor has already started invoking “bankruptcy protection” rhetoric. Without a fraud-based criminal result, your EPU title may become an ordinary pre-bankruptcy claim vulnerable to oddłużenie. With a final **art. 286** result and, best of all, an **art. 46** compensation order, the case changes from “recover while he is still exposed” to “the debt survives bankruptcy and can be pursued from future income and assets.” That difference is so large that it materially changes expected value even if the prosecution risk is far from zero. citeturn1view0turn44view0turn44view1turn27view1

It also changes settlement leverage. Before a conviction or art. 46 order, the debtor can rationally think: “If I can obtain consumer bankruptcy, the judgment may become economically useless.” After a conviction plus compensatory ruling, that logic weakens sharply, because bankruptcy no longer erases the debt. In other words, the criminal route is not valuable only for punishment; it changes the debtor’s payoff matrix by threatening a debt that survives his fresh start. citeturn1view0turn44view0turn10view0

The honest downside is timing and procedural friction. If you continue pushing the **same 155,000 PLN claim** in EPU, **art. 415 § 1 KPK** may prevent the criminal court from giving you the standalone art. 46 title for that same loss. And if a bankruptcy discharge order becomes final before the criminal route crystallizes into either an art. 46 compensation order or a final criminal establishment of the offense that you can deploy before discharge, the “nuclear option” weakens dramatically. So the criminal track is worth it, but it must be managed with deliberate sequencing rather than treated as a decorative parallel action. citeturn11view0turn30view0turn37view0

Finally, on the “mirror lever”: his **failure to file** or a later bankruptcy filing changes the discharge landscape, but not the art. 491^21 exception itself. In consumer bankruptcy, if the court concludes that the debtor intentionally caused insolvency or materially deepened it, the court may refuse any discharge under **art. 491^14a PU** unless equity or humanitarian grounds justify relief; if discharge is still granted, the repayment plan becomes longer under **art. 491^15 ust. 1a PU**. And if he later breaches plan duties, the court may revoke the plan under **art. 491^20 PU**, in which case **no debts are discharged at all**. Those are powerful anti-debtor levers, but they are separate from the specific shield that makes **your** claim non-dischargeable. Your best bankruptcy-proofing rule remains **art. 491^21 ust. 2 PU**, with **art. 46 KK** as the cleanest trigger. citeturn27view0turn27view1turn26view3

**Bottom line on your core question:** if you can obtain a **final art. 46 KK compensation order** on the 155,000 PLN, that amount should be treated as **non-dischargeable in Polish consumer bankruptcy even without a separate civil judgment**. A **mere civil judgment** without a final criminal establishment is much less secure as a shield. And because **art. 415 § 1 KPK** can block art. 46 when the same claim is already pending or adjudicated civilly, the value of the criminal track is highest when it is sequenced to produce the compensatory order before bankruptcy and before a civil title on the same loss forecloses it. citeturn44view0turn11view0turn1view0turn25view0
