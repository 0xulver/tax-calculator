# Enforcing an EPU Title Against a JDG When the Polish Bank Account Is Empty

## Practical frame

On the facts you gave, the debtor is not a company with a separate patrimony but a natural person running a sole proprietorship. In Polish terms, that means there is no corporate veil: once you have a **tytuł wykonawczy** against him, execution can be directed against **all parts of his property**, not just “business assets,” unless a specific statutory exemption applies. Biznes.gov.pl states that a sole proprietor bears unlimited liability with all of his assets, and KPC provides that a writ of execution supports recovery of the whole claim from all parts of the debtor’s property. citeturn23search0turn20view0

The key practical consequence is this: your enforcement request should be drafted against **Mateusz Szklarski as a natural person conducting business under the name GPUcomputer**, not as if GPUcomputer were a separate legal entity. Under KPC, a standard enforcement application already authorizes enforcement by all permissible methods **except real-estate execution**, which must be requested expressly. That is the correct starting posture for a case where the known Polish bank account may be empty and the real recovery target may be hidden Polish assets, receivables, or later cross-border enforcement. citeturn42view0

## From title to first enforcement acts

Once you have the final EPU payment order with a clause of enforceability, you file a **wniosek egzekucyjny** with a komornik and attach the **tytuł wykonawczy**. KPC requires the application to identify the performance sought and to include the enforceable title. The Ministry of Justice maintains a public “find a bailiff” tool, which is the cleanest official way to select the office. citeturn42view0turn11search6

For choice of komornik, **art. 10 of the Act on Court Bailiffs does give the creditor a choice**, but not nationwide. You may choose a bailiff **within the appellate-court area of the bailiff who would otherwise be competent under the KPC**. For a Kraków-centered enforcement picture, that means the **Kraków Court of Appeal area**, not Warsaw or Gdańsk. The choice right does **not** apply to execution from real estate, surrender of real estate, placement into possession, eviction, and analogous proceedings; those stay territorially fixed. If you use a bailiff “from choice,” you must include a written statement that you are exercising that right. A chosen bailiff may also refuse “choice” cases if statutory overload thresholds are met. citeturn41view0turn10search0

Why that choice matters in your scenario is mostly practical. As an inference from the statutes, if you expect mostly electronic bank and register work, another bailiff within the Kraków appellate area can be fine. But if you expect local field checks in Kraków, service issues tied to the Polish address, or coercive asset-list steps later, a locally competent Kraków bailiff is often cleaner, because out-of-rewir travel costs of a chosen bailiff are borne by the creditor and are not shifted onto the debtor, and a non-general-competence bailiff may have to transfer the matter if coercive asset-disclosure measures become necessary. citeturn41view0turn8view0turn20view0

In the application itself, the safest drafting is to ask for **all standard enforcement methods at once**, and to be very specific about identifiers: full name, business name, NIP, CEIDG address, any PESEL you have, and the Dutch residential/work address if known. You should explicitly name the known Polish bank account, because KPC provides that bank-account seizure becomes effective upon service to the bank and covers not only the balance present at that moment, but also later incoming funds and even funds paid into another account opened after the seizure. So even if the present balance is zero, naming the account is still worth doing immediately. citeturn42view0turn39view0

A strong first filing usually asks for: immediate seizure of the known bank account; a broad OGNIVO inquiry across participating Polish banks and credit unions; garnishment of any Polish receivables from counterparties; seizure of any tax refund; inquiries to ZUS and tax authorities; vehicle checks; seizure of movables if a real location is identified; and, if any real estate is discovered, a later direction to execution against it. Under KPC, if you did not indicate specific assets, the bailiff must still make a first-pass search from known proceedings and public electronic sources, and call on the debtor to submit a list of assets. citeturn11search0turn42view0turn5view0

## What the komornik can actually search and seize in Poland

For bank accounts, the practical tool is **OGNIVO**. KIR describes OGNIVO as the online communication system linking banks and SKOKs with authorized bodies, including judicial enforcement authorities. In practice, this is the quickest way for a bailiff to identify where the debtor maintains Polish accounts and then send electronic garnishments. Legally, bank-account execution is a distinct KPC mode of execution; the seizure is effective on service to the bank, and the bank must transmit seized funds immediately. citeturn11search0turn39view0

For vehicles, court bailiffs have electronic access to **CEPiK**. Gov.pl states that data access for bailiffs is handled electronically and that there is a dedicated access path and application form for court bailiffs. If a vehicle appears, the bailiff can move to seizure of that movable property. Movables generally are seized under the rules on execution from movables. citeturn17view0turn17view1turn39view2

For real estate, the public **Elektroniczne Księgi Wieczyste** system is important but has a limit: public inspection works **by land-register number**, not by debtor name. Gov.pl expressly says you need the electronic land-register number to view the contents. So in a case like yours, the first challenge is usually **finding** real estate, not reading the register once found. Once a property is identified, two different tools matter. First, real-estate execution itself must be run by the bailiff attached to the court where the property is located. Second, independently of an auction, a creditor whose claim is established by an enforceable title may obtain a **compulsory mortgage** on all of the debtor’s real estate. That can be strategically powerful because it gives you a secured foothold before an eventual sale. citeturn19search1turn14view0turn31view2

For institutional data and third-party receivables, KPC gives the bailiff broad information powers. Under **art. 761 § 1^1 KPC**, among the entities obliged to provide information needed for proper enforcement are tax authorities, pension authorities, banks, securities institutions, cooperative savings institutions, and other listed bodies. That is the legal basis for ZUS / tax-office / banking information requests. If the debtor has receivables against third parties, KPC allows garnishment of those receivables by notifying both the debtor and the third-party debtor and requiring the third party to state within a week whether and in what amount it owes the debtor. Tax refunds can also be seized specifically under the KPC rules on seizure of tax overpayments and refunds. citeturn4view0turn39view1

Wages are similar in principle, but only when the wage source is within the reach of Polish execution. KPC provides for seizure of employment income by notifying the employer and restricting the debtor’s ability to receive the attachable portion. But that mechanism is a Polish execution tool directed to the employer served in the enforcement process; it is not a direct bridge to a Dutch employer. That cross-border step needs a separate EU enforcement route, which I address at the end. citeturn39view3turn37view0turn35view0

## Costs, advances, and realistic timing

For an ordinary money-enforcement case, the creditor usually does **not** pay a large upfront percentage just to open the file. What you should expect instead are **advances** for expenses: correspondence, obtaining documents and information, travel if necessary, and similar out-of-pocket items. The costs statute says the bailiff may condition an expense-generating act on an advance, sets a general cap of 60 PLN for a single correspondence advance unless materially higher costs are expected, and allows the bailiff to charge the creditor for uncovered expenses if enforcement proves wholly or partly ineffective. If you choose a bailiff from outside the local rewir and he performs acts outside his rewir, travel, per diem, hotel, and related costs are on you and are not shifted to the debtor. citeturn8view0

One specific fixed fee matters a lot in empty-asset cases: **poszukiwanie majątku** under **art. 801^2 KPC** costs **100 PLN**. The important nuance is that the statute treats this as something distinct from ordinary information requests to the main institutional sources. In other words, a bailiff’s basic bank/ZUS/tax/register inquiries are not yet the special “asset search” order for which the 100 PLN fee is charged. As a practical matter, you should be prepared to order this promptly if the first pass finds nothing significant. citeturn20view0turn9view0

If the bailiff actually recovers money, the default enforcement fee in a money case is **10% of the amount recovered**, charged to the debtor. If the debtor pays the whole or part of the claim **to the bailiff** within one month from service of the notice of commencement, that fee drops to **3%** on the amount recovered that way. On a full recovery of 155,000 PLN, that means a 15,500 PLN fee at the ordinary rate, or 4,650 PLN if the debtor pays through the bailiff within that one-month window. citeturn26view0

The strategic trap is not the success fee but the fee exposure on discontinuance. If the creditor withdraws the case **before** the debtor has been served with notice of commencement, the fee is only **100 PLN**. If the case is later discontinued at the creditor’s request, or by operation of **art. 824 § 1 pkt 4 KPC** because the creditor has been inactive for six months, the standard rule is a **5% fee on the amount remaining to be recovered**. On your 155,000 PLN claim, that is 7,750 PLN. By contrast, if the case is discontinued because execution is **ineffective** under **art. 824 § 1 pkt 3 KPC**, the fee is generally **150 PLN**, plus any unrecovered expenses. In a no-asset case, that distinction is crucial: you want a formal “ineffectiveness” closure, not a creditor-inactivity closure. citeturn6view0turn5view2turn8view0

The statutes do not impose one single overall deadline for finishing a no-asset case, but they do give some key timing markers. Once an advance is paid, the act that required the advance should be taken within **7 days**. A bank seizure is effective upon service to the bank, and the bank must transfer seized funds **immediately**. A third-party debtor served with a receivable garnishment must answer within **one week**. In real life, that means the **first electronic sweep** can begin quickly, while a property-heavy case, especially one involving real estate, will take much longer. citeturn8view0turn39view0turn39view1

If a compulsory mortgage becomes available because you identify Polish real estate, the court fee for entry of a limited real right in the land register is generally **200 PLN**. That is usually modest compared with the leverage of obtaining security on real estate. citeturn14view0turn16view0

## What if there is nothing in Poland

If the known account is empty, CEPiK shows nothing useful, no real estate is identified, and institutional / third-party checks do not reveal attachable property even sufficient to cover costs, KPC points toward **umorzenie postępowania egzekucyjnego wobec bezskuteczności egzekucji** under **art. 824 § 1 pkt 3 KPC**. Before getting there, if the first pass fails, the bailiff may hear the creditor, and if you want the case to continue you must commission the statutory **asset search** under **art. 801^2 KPC**. If even then no attachable property is found, the file is discontinued for ineffectiveness. citeturn42view0turn5view2

The core document you receive is not some free-floating generic “certificate,” but the bailiff’s **postanowienie o umorzeniu**. The costs statute also requires the bailiff to set out the fees recovered and due in the discontinuance order or another final order ending the case. That order is the official record that Polish enforcement was tried and failed because there was no effective Polish asset base to reach. citeturn26view0turn26view2

That document has real strategic value. For income-tax purposes, both the PIT and CIT statutes recognize non-collectibility documented by a **postanowienie o nieściągalności** issued by the competent enforcement authority and accepted by the creditor as corresponding to the facts. Because of that statutory rule, a bailiff paper clearly recording failed collectibility can be highly valuable for tax recognition of a bad debt, although the exact accounting/tax treatment still depends on the creditor’s tax regime and on the wording of the enforcement document. I would treat that as a strong reason to make sure the discontinuance order clearly reflects ineffectiveness due to lack of collectible assets. citeturn30view0turn30view1

The same order is also useful in the **criminal track**, but with an important limit. Article 300 of the Penal Code criminalizes frustrating or reducing a creditor’s satisfaction by removing, hiding, selling, gifting, sham-encumbering, damaging, or otherwise dissipating assets in threatened insolvency, and also by doing so to frustrate execution of a court order when the assets are seized or threatened with seizure. An ineffectiveness order does **not** by itself prove an offense, but it can become strong supporting evidence when combined with asset transfers, sham disposals, or suspicious encumbrances around the time of the claim or execution. citeturn25view0

It also matters for later attempts. A claim established by a final court judgment or payment order generally prescribes after **six years**, while future periodic benefits such as post-judgment interest prescribe after **three years**. Acts taken before a court or other authority directly to enforce the claim interrupt prescription. So the title is not something that “dies quickly.” If the debtor later acquires Polish property, reopens business activity in Poland, receives a tax refund, or starts routing money back through a Polish bank, you can relaunch enforcement on the basis of the same title rather than starting a new merits lawsuit. citeturn2view0

A separate pressure tool is **wyjawienie majątku** under **art. 913 KPC**. This is a court procedure, not just a bailiff letter. The creditor may request it if the seized assets do not promise satisfaction, and in some circumstances even before full-blown enforcement if the creditor makes it probable that from the known attachable stream he will not recover the debt within six months. The application is filed with the court of the debtor’s general jurisdiction. The court can order the debtor to submit a full asset list and swear to its completeness. If the debtor does not appear, refuses to answer, or refuses to swear, the court may fine him, order compelled appearance, and even apply arrest for up to one month. citeturn5view3turn31view0turn31view1turn31view2

Can that be used while he is in the Netherlands? **Yes, but with weaker bite in practice.** Service of judicial documents across EU Member States is governed by Regulation (EU) 2020/1784, so the procedure itself is not blocked simply because the debtor is abroad. But the coercive teeth in art. 916 KPC are built around personal appearance before the Polish court and domestic coercion. So if the debtor remains physically in the Netherlands, the remedy is legally available and still useful for building a record and forcing formal disclosure attempts, but it is usually less immediately effective than it would be against a debtor physically present in Poland. That is an inference from the service regime and the Polish coercive measures. citeturn32search0turn32search4turn31view2

## Dutch wages and the fastest anti-dissipation move

At a high level, a **Polish komornik does not directly reach Dutch wages from Poland**. The EU rule is that a judgment enforceable in one Member State is enforceable in another Member State without a declaration of enforceability, but the **procedure of enforcement is governed by the law of the Member State addressed**. The Dutch e-Justice page explains that enforcement in the Netherlands is carried out there by a Dutch bailiff and that one of the standard execution methods is garnishment against a third party, including a wage attachment. So the real route to Dutch salary is: Polish enforceable judgment, EU certificate, then Dutch enforcement machinery. For uncontested claims, a **European Enforcement Order** may also be available as a simplified certificate route. citeturn37view0turn35view0turn38search1

So if the Polish side is empty, the Polish enforcement file still matters because it produces the official failure record and may still catch Polish assets later, but it does **not** substitute for the Dutch enforcement step. For prompt 6, the relevant instruments to analyze in depth are Brussels I Recast and, depending on the procedural fit, the European Enforcement Order, plus the Dutch wage-garnishment mechanics on the local side. citeturn37view0turn38search1

For anti-dissipation, the fastest **domestic** move after you have an enforceable order is usually **not** a separate Polish protective proceeding but filing enforcement immediately and broadly: seize the known bank account on day one, request OGNIVO inquiries, garnish specific receivables if you know counterparties, and trigger the first institutional checks. That matters because a known-bank seizure captures later incoming funds as well. If real estate is discovered, move at once for a **compulsory mortgage**, because your enforceable title supports it and it is often the quickest way to lock in a secured position against Polish real estate. citeturn39view0turn14view0

If the immediate dissipation risk is not Polish assets but **bank accounts elsewhere in the EU**, the relevant fast-freeze instrument is the **European Account Preservation Order**. Regulation 655/2014 creates a Union procedure to preserve funds in a bank account maintained in another Member State, and it is available both before and after judgment. Its scope is bank accounts, however, **not wages**. So in your fact pattern, the fastest post-nakaz protective step is: immediate Polish execution for anything in Poland, and if you identify foreign EU bank accounts, consider the EAPO route; Dutch salary still requires the separate Dutch enforcement path. citeturn36view3turn33search2
