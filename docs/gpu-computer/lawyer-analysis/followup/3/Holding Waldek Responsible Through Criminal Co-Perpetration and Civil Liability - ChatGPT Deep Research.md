# Holding Waldek Responsible Through Criminal Co-Perpetration and Civil Liability

## Overview

On the facts you describe, there **is** a legally coherent bridge from a criminal case against Waldemar Łopata to personal civil recovery against him. The bridge is not “because he was involved in the business,” but because Polish law separately recognizes liability for the person who **actually perpetrated, co-perpetrated, directed, instigated, helped, or knowingly benefited from** the fraudulent act. In other words, the fact that the formal invoice and bank account were Mateusz Szklarski’s matters a great deal for the **contract** claim, but it does **not** by itself block criminal liability under the Penal Code or tort liability under the Civil Code. citeturn39view0turn11view0turn39view2turn37view0

The biggest caution is temporal. For fraud under Article 286 § 1 KK, the prosecution must prove a **direct fraudulent intent** tied to the moment of the victim’s unfavorable disposition of property. Polish case law is consistently clear that not every failed commercial deal or non-performance of a contract is fraud; the fraudulent intent must exist when the obligation is undertaken or, at the latest, when the deceptive act causes the payment. Later lies, contradictory stories, and fake refund promises are often very important evidence of guilty knowledge and concealment, but **standing alone** they do not automatically prove that the original payment was induced by fraud. citeturn14view0turn14view1turn14view2turn13view1

That said, your fact pattern does not read like “mere later customer-service handling.” If Waldek was the person who actually ran the communication channels, controlled both business mailboxes, gave the operative explanations, handled the refund promises, and functioned as the day-to-day operator while Mateusz was merely the formal JDG holder, that is exactly the type of factual matrix from which a prosecutor may build either **co-perpetration** or **directing perpetration** under Article 18 § 1 KK. If the evidence ultimately falls short of that, the same facts can still support **aiding** under Article 18 § 3 KK or, less commonly, **instigation** under Article 18 § 2 KK. citeturn39view0turn10view0turn10view2turn7view2turn7view4

On the civil side, the lawyer’s “no basis against Waldek at this stage because you contracted with Mateusz’s firm” objection is understandable as a **contract-privity** point, but it is not the whole picture. If Waldek is shown to have personally participated in the deceit, your route against him is no longer a contract claim at all; it becomes a **delict/tort** claim. Under Polish law, the existence of a contractual relationship with one defendant does **not** exclude a tort claim arising out of the same facts, and several tortfeasors are solidarily liable. If Waldek was not the direct co-perpetrator but instead induced, assisted, or knowingly profited from the fraud, Article 422 KC opens a further delict route. citeturn29view2turn39view2turn37view0

## Criminal analysis

### Fraud under Article 286 as the underlying offense

Article 286 § 1 KK covers the person who, for financial gain, causes another person to make an unfavorable disposition of property by misleading them, exploiting an error, or exploiting their inability to properly understand the act. Courts repeatedly emphasize that “misleading” is broad: the statute does not require some unusually sophisticated trick. But the same case law is just as clear that Article 286 is an intentional, purpose-driven offense; there must be proof that the actor wanted to achieve a financial benefit through deception causing the disposition of property. citeturn11view0turn14view1turn14view2

That matters because your strongest criminal theory against Waldek depends on showing that his role was not limited to “after the payment everything started going wrong.” If his involvement can be tied to the **pre-payment sales phase**—for example, specifications, availability assurances, delivery timing, urgency, supplier claims, customs claims, or approval of the transaction terms—then his conduct goes directly to the deception element of Article 286. If, by contrast, the only provable acts attributable to him begin **after** the PLN 155,000 transfer, then his criminal significance remains real, but the theory shifts more toward concealment, continuation of the scheme, operational control, benefit, or helping another offender rather than straightforward authorship of the initial deception. citeturn14view0turn14view1turn14view2

### Co-perpetration and directing perpetration under Article 18 § 1 KK

Article 18 § 1 KK provides that a perpetrator is not only the person who commits the prohibited act personally, but also the one who commits it **jointly and in agreement** with another person, and also the one who **directs** another person’s commission of the act. That statutory structure is exactly why the formal invoice issuer and the actual operator do not have to be the same person. citeturn39view0

The Supreme Court’s co-perpetration jurisprudence matters here. It explains that “jointly” has an objective side: the offense may be realized through **complementary conduct** of different participants; a co-perpetrator need not personally perform every statutory element. The Court also stresses, however, that courts must identify **concrete conduct** by the accused that significantly or necessarily conditioned the direct realization of the offense by others. The distinction between co-perpetration and mere assistance therefore turns on the significance of the contribution, the existence of an agreement, and whether the person treated the act as **their own common enterprise** rather than someone else’s act. citeturn10view0turn10view2

The “panowanie nad czynem” line of analysis points in the same direction: co-perpetration is associated with deciding on the undertaking of the prohibited act and its real course, with the co-perpetrator occupying a central rather than secondary position in the realization. citeturn7view2

Applied to your facts, the following are the most relevant indicators of potential Article 18 § 1 liability for Waldek:

If Waldek actually controlled **both** `waldek@gpucomputer.pl` and the root `gpucomputer@gpucomputer.pl` mailbox, and if those mailboxes were core channels through which the deal and later assurances were conducted, that is strong evidence that he was not an incidental intermediary but someone exercising operational control over the transaction narrative. On its own, mailbox control does not prove fraud, but it is highly relevant to authorship, role differentiation, and “who was really running this.” citeturn39view0turn10view0turn10view2

If the near-identical refund messages sent one minute apart from both mailboxes are authentic and can be supported by full headers or server logs, they strongly support the inference that the two accounts were being run by the same person or under the same direction. Again, the legal importance is not the duplication itself; it is what the duplication says about **control of the business communication infrastructure** and therefore about who had “panowanie” over the operative execution of the scheme. citeturn39view0turn7view2turn10view0

If Waldek personally gave the contradictory explanations—“customs cleared the goods” versus “the Hong Kong supplier went bankrupt and the parts never arrived”—that helps in two ways. First, it is classic impeachment material showing consciousness of falsehood or at least lack of a truthful, stable account. Second, it can help prove he was the person actually managing the business story presented to you. The weakness is that these statements appear to be **post-payment**, so they are better evidence of knowing participation and concealment than self-sufficient proof of the original Article 286 intent. citeturn11view0turn14view0turn14view2

If it can be shown that Waldek was the **de facto operator** of GPUcomputer while Mateusz lived and worked abroad full time, then the “formal front / actual operator” theory becomes materially stronger. That does not prove co-perpetration by itself, but it fits a very standard prosecutorial narrative: one person is the nominal contractor and account-holder, another actually runs the transaction, makes the representations, and controls the responses. In that configuration, ordinary co-perpetration is already plausible; **directing perpetration** becomes plausible if the evidence shows that Mateusz’s role was largely formal and that Waldek was calling the shots. citeturn39view0turn10view0turn10view2turn7view2

### Whether Waldek can be the deceiver even if Mateusz issued the invoice

Yes—**in principle**. Article 286 focuses on **who caused the victim’s unfavorable disposition of property through deception**, not on whose name appears on the proforma invoice or bank account. Courts describe the offense in terms of the person who misleads and thereby causes the disposition. They do not make invoice issuance a constitutive element of the offense. citeturn11view0turn14view2

So, if the evidence shows that Waldek was the person who actually made the factual claims that induced payment, controlled the business mailboxes through which those claims were communicated, or significantly conditioned the fraudulent transaction’s realization, he can be charged as a perpetrator or co-perpetrator **independently** of the fact that the invoice and bank account were in Mateusz’s JDG. What the invoice/account arrangement mainly does is create a competing inference that Mateusz also participated, and it may make the contract claim against Mateusz’s firm simpler. It does not, by itself, legally exclude Waldek from being treated as a perpetrator of deception. citeturn39view0turn10view2turn11view0

### Fallback theories under Article 18 §§ 2–3 and the role of Article 21 § 2

If the prosecutor cannot prove that Waldek and Mateusz acted “jointly and in agreement” with the level of significance required for co-perpetration, Article 18 still leaves fallback routes.

Under Article 18 § 2 KK, **instigation** requires that a person, wanting another person to commit the prohibited act, induces them to do so. The statutory wording “chcąc” is strong: this is a direct-intent form. In your case, that would fit a theory that Waldek recruited or instructed Mateusz to act as the formal entrepreneur/account-holder. That is possible, but on your current facts it looks less natural than co-perpetration or assistance because it would require proof of a concrete act of inducement. citeturn39view0

Under Article 18 § 3 KK, **aiding** is broader. It covers the person who, with intent that another commit the offense, facilitates it—for example by providing information, channels, advice, or other practical means. The Supreme Court has expressly stated that assistance under Article 18 § 3 may be committed with **direct or eventual intent**, unlike instigation. That makes this fallback especially useful if the evidence ultimately shows that Waldek ran the communication channels, provided the false stories, stabilized the victim’s trust, or otherwise made the fraud easier, but the prosecutor cannot prove the stronger “common enterprise / control over the act” necessary for Article 18 § 1. citeturn39view0turn7view4

Article 20 KK is also important in practice: each participant answers **within the limits of their own intent or negligence**, independently of the liability of the others. So even if Mateusz seeks to minimize his role or the prosecution struggles to prove the full depth of his intent, that does not preclude separate liability for Waldek if Waldek’s own state of mind and conduct are established. citeturn39view1

Article 21 § 2 KK is, in this case, secondary rather than central. It deals with a personal circumstance of the perpetrator that affects punishability and also forms part of the offense’s statutory elements; a co-actor can then be liable for that aggravated form if they knew of the circumstance. Fraud under Article 286 § 1 is framed as a **common** offense—“kto”—and does not require a special personal status such as being the invoice issuer, account-holder, entrepreneur, or VAT-registered trader. So the fact that Mateusz was the formal JDG holder is **not** the sort of personal circumstance that shields Waldek. In this setting, Article 21 mostly confirms a broader principle: individual personal circumstances are not automatically transferred, but where a special circumstance forms part of the offense and the co-actor knows it, it can matter. On your facts, the key issue is not Article 21; it is proof of Waldek’s own role and knowledge. citeturn39view1turn11view0

## The civil bridge

### How a criminal finding turns into a civil claim against Waldek

The cleanest bridge is this:

If Waldek is found to have been a **direct co-perpetrator** of the fraud, the civil basis against him is primarily **Article 415 KC**—his own tortious act—combined with **Article 441 § 1 KC**, under which multiple persons responsible for damage caused by a tort are **solidarily liable**. A criminal co-perpetration finding is therefore a very strong platform for saying: Waldek is personally liable for the whole PLN 155,000, and you may claim the full amount from him, from Mateusz, or from both until you are paid. citeturn29view2turn37view0

If the criminal finding is weaker—say, Waldek is proved not as a co-perpetrator but as someone who **instigated**, **helped**, or **knowingly benefited**—then **Article 422 KC** becomes central. That provision makes liable not only the person who directly caused the damage, but also the person who induced it, assisted it, or knowingly profited from it. Once liability is established on that basis, Article 441 § 1 KC supplies solidarity. citeturn39view2turn16view0turn16view2turn37view0

This is exactly how the lawyer’s “you only contracted with Mateusz’s firm” objection gets pierced. Contract privity is decisive for the **contract** claim; it is not decisive for the **tort** claim. Article 443 KC expressly states that the fact that the damaging conduct also constituted non-performance or improper performance of a pre-existing obligation does **not** exclude a tort claim, unless the content of that obligation indicates otherwise. So if the same facts amount both to non-performance by Mateusz’s firm and to a fraudulent delict by Waldek, Polish law allows the delict route to run alongside the contractual route. citeturn29view2turn37view0

### The limits of the bridge if Waldek is not convicted

This is where the nuances matter. A conviction of **Mateusz alone** does not automatically make Waldek civilly liable. Article 11 KPC binds the civil court with respect to the commission of the offense established in a **final criminal conviction**, but the binding force extends only to the offense as described in the operative part of the criminal judgment, and a person who was **not** an accused in the criminal case may still rely on all circumstances excluding or limiting their own civil liability. Supreme Court case law is very clear on this point. citeturn40view0turn18view1turn18view2turn20view0

That means the bridge works at different strength levels:

If **Waldek himself is convicted**, the later civil case against him becomes much easier. The civil court is bound by the criminal judgment as to the offense’s commission within the scope of the operative findings, and Waldek cannot simply re-litigate that the crime did not happen. citeturn40view0turn18view1turn18view2

If **only Mateusz is convicted**, the conviction still helps you evidentially, but it is **not a full prejudykat against Waldek**. The Supreme Court has stressed that the criminal judgment does not bind civil courts regarding the conduct, knowledge, or awareness of a person who was not accused and convicted in the criminal case. That is exactly why the criminal case against Waldek personally is such an important bridge. citeturn20view0turn18view2

### Restitution under Article 46 KK

Article 46 § 1 KK is a direct compensatory tool. Upon conviction, the criminal court may order—and on the victim’s motion must order—repair of the damage, applying civil-law rules. Since 2015, this compensatory measure explicitly operates with civil-law principles, which means that where several participants are responsible for damage caused by a criminally tortious act, the court may order repair **solidarily**. The Supreme Court and appellate courts have repeatedly accepted that solidarity is available in this setting. citeturn41view0turn22view1turn22view2

For your purposes, this is the strongest criminal-to-civil bridge of all. If Waldek is convicted as co-perpetrator, the criminal court can order him personally to pay the damage; in an appropriate case that can be done jointly and severally with the other perpetrator. Article 46 § 3 KK then makes clear that obtaining a criminal restitution order does not bar you from seeking the still-unpaid balance in civil proceedings. citeturn41view0turn22view2

### Prejudykat under Article 11 KPC

Article 11 KPC is narrower than many litigants assume, but still very powerful.

The civil court is bound by the **final criminal conviction** only as to the offense’s commission, and only within what was affirmatively established in the **operative part** of the conviction. It is not bound by every statement in the criminal judgment’s reasoning. Supreme Court case law stresses exactly that distinction: the operative description of the offense binds; broader remarks in the reasoning do not automatically do so. citeturn40view0turn18view1

That is why, in a case like yours, the ideal criminal outcome is not merely “someone gets convicted,” but rather: **Waldek is convicted in a way that the operative part of the judgment captures his role in the deception and the damage.** If that happens, the later civil suit becomes far more targeted and efficient. If he is not convicted, you can still sue him civilly, but then Article 11 KPC does not carry you nearly as far. citeturn40view0turn18view2turn20view0

A final civil-side advantage is limitation. If the damage resulted from a felony or misdemeanor offense, Article 442¹ § 2 KC gives a **20-year** limitation period for the tort claim, counted from the commission of the offense. That can matter if the civil route against Waldek is delayed while the criminal case develops. citeturn37view0

## Can Waldek be reached without a criminal conviction

Yes, but the theories are not equally strong.

The strongest non-conviction route is a direct **tort/deceit** claim. If you can prove in civil court that Waldek personally made false factual representations that induced the transfer, then Article 415 KC gives you the base, Article 441 § 1 KC gives solidarity if Mateusz or others are also liable, and Article 443 KC answers the “but you had a contract with the firm” objection. On your facts, I would rate this theory as **medium now, potentially strong later**, depending almost entirely on how much authenticated **pre-payment** conduct by Waldek you can show. citeturn29view2turn37view0turn38view0

The second route is **Article 422 KC**. This is strongest if the eventual findings show one of three things: that Waldek induced the fraud, that he materially helped it, or that he knowingly benefited from it. The Supreme Court has described Article 422 as an independent delict basis for instigation, assistance, and conscious benefit, and has also held that those liable under Article 422 are solidarily liable with the direct wrongdoer under Article 441 § 1 KC. citeturn16view0

But there are two important limits. First, for the “knowingly benefited” branch, the Supreme Court has held that the beneficiary must have **positive knowledge** that he is benefiting from another’s wrongful act; mere suspicion is not enough. Second, Article 422 does **not** extend indefinitely to all after-the-fact cover-up behavior. The Court has said that a person who merely helped conceal damage **after it had already been caused** is not within Article 422 on that basis alone, unless the support was promised beforehand or the person consciously benefited from the damage. That limit is very relevant to your post-payment refund-promise evidence. citeturn16view2turn16view0

A third route is **unjust enrichment**. Articles 405 and 410 KC allow recovery from a person who, without legal basis, obtained a benefit at another’s expense, including by way of an undue payment; Article 407 extends liability to a gratuitous transferee if the original recipient transferred the enrichment on for free. As a claim against Waldek, however, unjust enrichment is only as good as your tracing evidence. If the PLN 155,000 stayed with Mateusz’s JDG and never enriched Waldek, the theory is weak. If bank records show transfers to Waldek, payment of his debts, acquisition of assets for him, or a gratuitous onward transfer, it becomes much more serious. On your current facts, I would rate unjust enrichment as **low to medium**, contingent on bank tracing. citeturn29view0turn29view1turn36view0

A fourth possibility—often raised in practice but weaker here—is the **falsus procurator** route under Article 103 KC. That provision makes the unauthorized representative personally liable if a contract made in another’s name is not confirmed. Here, however, the arrangement you describe does not primarily look like an unconfirmed unauthorized act. The firm issued the invoice and accepted payment, which in practice points toward confirmation/ratification rather than the absence of it. So Article 103 is doctrinally available in Polish law, but on your facts it is **not** the theory I would prioritize. citeturn31view0turn31view1

As for **firmanctwo**, Article 55 KKS defines a tax-fiscal offense where a taxpayer uses another entity’s name or firm to conceal that they are really running the business or to conceal its real scale. That concept may be useful narratively if the evidence suggests that Mateusz’s JDG was a formal shell while Waldek was the actual operator. But firmanctwo is not, by itself, a ready-made private-law cause of action for recovering your PLN 155,000. It is best seen as a **supporting factual pattern** for criminal/tax authorities, not as a standalone civil recovery theory. citeturn27view1turn27view2

The phrase “działanie faktyczne” by itself does not solve the problem either. Polish private law does not award you PLN 155,000 simply because someone was the “real operator.” The de facto role matters because it helps prove a recognized head of liability—most importantly **tort**, **aiding/benefit under Article 422**, or **enrichment**. citeturn29view2turn39view2turn29view0

## Should the notice of crime name Waldek now

Yes. My recommendation is to **name Waldek from the outset**, but to do so in a disciplined way: not as an already-proved villain, but as a **factually grounded suspected co-perpetrator, alternatively directing perpetrator, and in the further alternative instigator or helper**.

That recommendation follows from the structure of preparatory proceedings. The purpose of the investigation is to determine whether a crime occurred, detect the perpetrator, identify victims and the scale of damage, and secure evidence. Proceedings are opened once there is justified suspicion of a crime, and person-specific charges follow when the data sufficiently justify suspicion against a specified person. The prosecutor is not bound by your legal classification and may later modify the charges as evidence develops. citeturn34view0turn34view1turn33view2

Naming Waldek now has three concrete advantages.

First, it helps prevent the investigation from collapsing too quickly into a one-dimensional “invoice issuer only” narrative. Your evidence, as you describe it, already points beyond the formal JDG holder. Bringing Waldek in from the beginning makes it more likely that the prosecutor will seek the right digital evidence early: mailbox logs, hosting records, device data, supplier correspondence, and traces of who actually ran the transaction. That is especially important where electronic evidence may disappear or be overwritten. citeturn34view0turn33view2

Second, naming him now increases the odds that, if the evidence justifies it, the criminal case can later produce the **stronger civil bridge**—a conviction of Waldek himself and possibly an Article 46 restitution order against him. If he is absent from the case entirely, you may still use the investigation evidentially later, but you lose the most efficient prejudykat/restitution path. citeturn41view0turn40view0turn20view0

Third, even if the prosecutor initially frames the matter more cautiously, there is no downside in giving them a structured alternative qualification. Article 314 KPK allows the authorities to adjust the act description and legal qualification if the evidentiary picture changes. So you are not “overcommitting” the case by identifying Waldek now; you are helping the prosecution preserve the option. citeturn33view2

The genuine downside is rhetorical, not legal. If the report of crime states as a certainty what is still only an inference—for example, kinship, precise control over all systems, or a fully formed common plan without proof—it can make the filing look overstated. The better approach is to say, in substance: **the evidence already points to Waldemar Łopata as the real operator of the dealings and therefore at least a suspected co-perpetrator; the prosecution should verify his role through specific evidence-gathering steps.** That is firm without being reckless. citeturn34view1turn33view2

As the injured party, you should also think of the notice not as a single narrative but as an evidence-preservation request. The filing should ask the prosecutor to verify, among other things, who controlled the mailboxes and website, who actually negotiated before payment, whether any real order or customs/supplier chain existed, and where the PLN 155,000 went after receipt. As an injured party, you may also submit motions for investigative acts during the preparatory proceedings. citeturn33view2turn34view1

## Bottom line

The most accurate way to put it is this:

Your lawyer is right that **contractually** you dealt with Mateusz’s JDG, so a clean contract claim lies there first. But if the criminal evidence shows that Waldek actually ran the deception, then the case against him stops being a contract case and becomes a **tort/crime-participation case**. That is the bridge.

For the criminal side, the best theory is **Article 18 § 1 KK**—ordinary co-perpetration, and possibly directing perpetration if the evidence shows Waldek used Mateusz as a formal front while personally directing the transaction. Your mailbox-control evidence, refund-promise authorship, contradictory stories, long-term business branding, and de facto operational role all point in that direction. The key missing piece is proof tying Waldek to the **pre-payment** deception and to the decision-making that caused the transfer. If that proof remains incomplete, Article 18 § 3 KK assistance is the natural fallback. citeturn39view0turn10view0turn10view2turn7view4

For the civil side, a criminal conviction of Waldek would be extremely valuable because it opens **Article 46 KK restitution**, and later a civil claim can rely on the conviction as a **prejudykat** within the limits of Article 11 KPC.
