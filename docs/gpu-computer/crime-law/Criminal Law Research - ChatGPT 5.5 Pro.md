I used your attached brief as the governing specification: you are assessing whether a criminal track can add leverage, asset-freeze potential, or pressure in parallel with the EPU/civil claim for a 155,000 PLN prepayment, non-delivery, written refund promise, and non-refund by a Polish JDG seller. 

My practical conclusion is:

5. **Criminal asset freezing is possible but not automatic.** It becomes realistic only if the prosecutor opens the case, identifies a suspect/accused, and sees a risk that compensation or fines will be unenforceable. ([Isap][2])
6. **Continue the civil track.** The criminal route should be treated as pressure and evidence-access leverage, not the primary recovery mechanism.

# A. Statutory offences and legal routes

## A1. Art. 286 § 1 KK — fraud / *oszustwo*

**Polish statutory text**

> „Kto, w celu osiągnięcia korzyści majątkowej, doprowadza inną osobę do niekorzystnego rozporządzenia własnym lub cudzym mieniem za pomocą wprowadzenia jej w błąd albo wyzyskania błędu lub niezdolności do należytego pojmowania przedsiębranego działania, podlega karze pozbawienia wolności od 6 miesięcy do lat 8.” ([Isap][3])

**English translation**

Whoever, for the purpose of obtaining a financial benefit, causes another person to make an unfavourable disposition of their own or another person’s property by misleading them, exploiting their mistake, or exploiting their inability properly to understand the action undertaken, is subject to imprisonment from 6 months to 8 years.

**Elements to prove**

The prosecutor must prove:

1. the debtor wanted to obtain a financial benefit;
2. you disposed of property — paid 155,000 PLN;
3. the disposition was unfavourable to you;
4. the payment was caused by deception, exploitation of mistake, or exploitation of inability to understand;
5. the relevant deception existed **at or before the moment you paid**, not only later;
6. causation: had you known the true facts, you would not have paid or would have paid on different terms.

**Penalty and limitation**

The basic penalty is **6 months to 8 years’ imprisonment**. Because the maximum exceeds 5 years, the ordinary limitation period is **15 years**, with possible extension rules once proceedings are initiated against a person. ([Isap][3])

The aggravated “property of significant value” threshold is **over 200,000 PLN**; your single 155,000 PLN claim is below that threshold unless the prosecution aggregates multiple victims or transactions into a broader fraud pattern. ([Isap][3])

**Prosecution mode**

This is generally a **public / ex officio offence**. A motion is required only in the special case of fraud committed to the detriment of a “nearest person,” which does not fit your facts. ([Isap][3])

**Fit to your facts: Moderate as a complaint; weak-to-moderate as a charge on current evidence**

Your facts justify asking the prosecutor to investigate art. 286 KK. The amount is substantial, the seller took full prepayment, failed to deliver, agreed to refund, did not refund, and gave an explanation involving forwarding funds to a foreign supplier while claiming bankruptcy protection that is not publicly confirmed in KRZ. 

But the current evidence does **not yet prove fraud**. Polish law does not automatically criminalise non-performance. The key missing issue is the seller’s state of knowledge and intent when he accepted your money. The Supreme Court has stressed that a mere advance payment, failure to perform, and failure to refund can be contractual liability rather than art. 286 fraud unless deception is proven. ([Supreme Court][1])

**Facts that would make art. 286 stronger**

Art. 286 becomes stronger if investigation shows that, before taking your payment, the debtor:

* already knew the Hong Kong supplier was unreliable, insolvent, fake, or not going to deliver;
* had no realistic path to deliver the ordered computer;
* was already insolvent and using new customer money to cover old obligations;
* kept accepting large prepayments after knowing earlier customers could not be supplied or refunded;
* falsely represented that goods were available, ordered, insured, secured, or refundable;
* did not transfer your funds to the supplier despite saying he did;
* diverted funds to personal expenses, selected creditors, or unrelated liabilities.

**Practical charging theory**

The complaint should say:

> There is a reasonable suspicion that the seller induced me to transfer 155,000 PLN by creating the impression that he was able and willing to deliver a custom computer or refund the price, while concealing material information about the true supplier risk, use of funds, solvency, and ability to perform.

Do **not** say, without proof, “he definitely committed fraud.” Say: “the facts justify investigation under art. 286 § 1 KK.”

## A2. Art. 300 KK — frustrating creditor satisfaction

**Polish statutory core**

Art. 300 KK covers a debtor who, while threatened with insolvency or bankruptcy, removes, hides, sells, donates, destroys, encumbers, or damages assets, thereby frustrating or reducing creditor satisfaction. Art. 300 § 2 separately covers asset actions taken to frustrate execution of a court or other state-authority decision. Penalties range from up to 3 years, to 3 months–5 years, and in many-creditor situations 6 months–8 years. ([Isap][3])

**English translation**

A debtor who, while threatened with insolvency or bankruptcy, disposes of or conceals assets in a way that prevents or reduces satisfaction of creditors may be criminally liable. If the conduct is aimed at frustrating enforcement of a court or state-authority decision, the offence is more serious.

**Elements to prove**

For art. 300 § 1:

1. the debtor is threatened with insolvency or bankruptcy;
2. the debtor removes, hides, sells, donates, destroys, burdens, or damages assets;
3. the action frustrates or reduces creditor satisfaction;
4. there is at least one creditor; many-creditor situations can aggravate.

For art. 300 § 2:

1. there is an actual or foreseeable court/state-authority decision or enforcement route;
2. the debtor acts to frustrate that enforcement;
3. the debtor targets assets that are or may be subject to execution.

**Penalty and limitation**

Depending on subsection, penalties include up to 3 years, 3 months–5 years, or 6 months–8 years. Limitation will generally be 5, 10, or 15 years depending on the maximum penalty for the subsection. ([Isap][3])

**Prosecution mode**

Art. 300 § 1 is prosecuted on the injured party’s motion when the injured party is not the State Treasury. Other forms may be prosecuted ex officio depending on subsection and facts. ([Isap][3])

**Fit to your facts: Weak now; moderate if asset concealment appears**

Right now you have a debt and non-payment, but not yet proof that the debtor hid, transferred, encumbered, or dissipated assets. Art. 300 becomes materially stronger if you obtain an enforceable civil title, enforcement begins, and the debtor then moves assets, empties accounts, shifts inventory, transfers business to relatives/another entity, or gives suspicious security to selected creditors.

**How to plead it**

In the complaint, do not assert art. 300 as proven. Ask the prosecutor to investigate whether, after taking customer funds and while claiming inability to refund, the debtor transferred, concealed, encumbered, or preferentially disposed of assets to frustrate creditors.

## A3. Art. 301 KK — causing insolvency / bankruptcy; reckless insolvency

**Polish statutory core**

Art. 301 KK covers, among other things: transferring assets to a newly created entity to damage many creditors; a debtor of several creditors bringing about bankruptcy or insolvency; and a debtor of several creditors who **recklessly** brings about bankruptcy or insolvency, especially by wasting assets, contracting obligations, or making transactions obviously contrary to prudent business principles. ([Isap][3])

**English translation**

A debtor with several creditors who causes insolvency or bankruptcy, including recklessly through wasteful or obviously imprudent transactions, may be criminally liable.

**Elements to prove**

For the most relevant form, art. 301 § 3:

1. the debtor has several creditors;
2. the debtor becomes insolvent or bankrupt;
3. the insolvency is caused recklessly;
4. the recklessness may consist of wasting assets, taking on obligations, or entering transactions obviously contrary to prudent business principles.

**Penalty and limitation**

The reckless form carries a fine, restriction of liberty, or imprisonment up to 2 years. Other forms carry heavier penalties, including 3 months–5 years. ([Isap][3])

**Fit to your facts: Potentially important, but evidence-dependent**

This is the closest Polish criminal provision to your “reckless handling of customer money” intuition. However, it is not a general customer-prepayment protection rule. It requires broader insolvency facts, especially **several creditors** and causation between reckless business conduct and insolvency.

If evidence shows he took multiple large prepayments, forwarded funds abroad without realistic safeguards, had no reserves, and thereby became insolvent, art. 301 § 3 becomes a serious investigative direction.

**Current fit: Weak-to-moderate**

Your single case alone is probably insufficient. It becomes stronger if other victims, accounting records, creditor lists, or bank records show a pattern.

## A4. Art. 302 KK — preferential payment / creditor bribery

**Polish statutory core**

Art. 302 § 1 covers a debtor who, while threatened with insolvency or bankruptcy and unable to satisfy all creditors, pays or secures only some creditors, thereby acting to the detriment of the others. Art. 302 §§ 2–3 cover corruption-like conduct involving creditors in bankruptcy or restructuring-style proceedings. ([Isap][3])

**English translation**

A debtor who cannot pay all creditors and, in the face of insolvency or bankruptcy, favours selected creditors to the detriment of others may be criminally liable.

**Penalty**

Art. 302 § 1 carries a fine, restriction of liberty, or imprisonment up to 2 years. The bribery-related forms carry up to 3 years. ([Isap][3])

**Fit to your facts: Weak unless selective payments are discovered**

This becomes relevant if he paid family, insiders, a preferred supplier, or selected creditors after knowing he could not refund you and others. You do not yet have that evidence.

**How to use it**

Ask the prosecutor to examine bank records and creditor payments after your prepayment and after refund obligations arose.

## A5. Art. 296 KK — abuse of trust / mismanagement

**Polish statutory text, core**

Art. 296 § 1 applies to a person who, being obliged by statute, decision, or contract to manage the property or business affairs of a natural person, legal person, or organisational unit, abuses powers or fails duties and causes significant property damage. Basic penalty: 3 months–5 years. More serious forms include acting for gain, causing great-value damage, and negligent forms. ([Isap][3])

**English translation**

A person entrusted with managing another person’s property or business affairs may be criminally liable if they abuse their powers or fail their duties and cause significant property damage.

**Fit to your facts: Not applicable / very weak**

This is probably **not** your article. A seller in an ordinary sale does not usually “manage” the customer’s property. Once the price is paid under a sales contract, the money normally becomes the seller’s property, subject to the seller’s civil obligation to deliver or refund. There is no obvious fiduciary or management mandate over your assets.

Also, “significant property damage” in KK terminology is tied to the **over 200,000 PLN** threshold; your single 155,000 PLN claim is below that threshold. ([Isap][3])

Art. 296 would become more plausible only if there were a special escrow, trust-like arrangement, mandate, agency relationship, or a managed fund structure. Your facts describe an ordinary merchant sale, not a fiduciary asset-management relationship.

# B. The “reckless handling of customer money” question

## Clear answer

**There does not appear to be a standalone Polish criminal offence that says an ordinary merchant who takes a large prepayment must segregate, escrow, insure, or guarantee that customer money before ordering goods from a supplier.**

For an ordinary merchant sale, the criminal law generally does not punish “bad commercial risk management” by itself. It punishes **deception**, **asset concealment**, **reckless insolvency involving several creditors**, **mismanagement of entrusted property**, **tax/invoice offences**, or **unfair commercial practices** in civil/administrative regimes.

So your intuition has a partial legal basis, but not as a simple rule: “large prepayment + risky supplier transfer = crime.”

## Specific answers

**Does Polish law impose a criminal duty to segregate or escrow merchant prepayments?**
Generally, no, not for a standard sale of goods. There may be special regimes in some industries or special contractual arrangements, but an ordinary computer merchant is not automatically a trustee of the price.

**Does custom-built vs. off-the-shelf matter?**
Not much for criminal law. It may matter for consumer withdrawal rights or factual reliance, but art. 286 KK still turns on deception and intent at the time of payment. Custom-built goods may make the supplier-risk story more plausible, but also make it more important whether the seller honestly disclosed procurement risk.

**Does it matter whether you were told the money would be forwarded to a foreign supplier?**
Yes. If you were clearly told: “I will send this to a Hong Kong supplier and if that supplier fails I cannot refund you,” fraud is harder. If you were led to believe you were buying from a Polish merchant who bore seller risk and would deliver/refund, while he knew he could not realistically do so, fraud becomes more plausible.

**Can undisclosed supplier risk be “introduction in error” under art. 286 KK?**
Potentially, yes, but only if the omission created a materially false impression and the seller intended to obtain payment through that false impression. The legal hook is not “risk existed,” but “the seller intentionally concealed material facts that would have changed your payment decision.”

**Is breach of consumer duty to inform automatically criminal?**
No. Consumer-law information duties can support civil, administrative, or UOKiK action. They may also be evidence of deception in a criminal fraud case, but they do not automatically convert the matter into art. 286 KK. UOKiK’s public-interest competence and misleading-omission concept are administrative/consumer-protection tools, not individual criminal recovery tools. ([Urząd Ochrony Konkurencji i Konsumentów][6])

**Is there a misdemeanour-level fallback under the Kodeks wykroczeń?**
I do not see a clean Kodeks wykroczeń offence for this exact fact pattern. Some unfair-market-practice regimes contain misdemeanour-style sanctions for aggressive practices, but your core facts are not an obvious aggressive-practice case. The better routes are art. 286 KK, arts. 300–302/301 § 3 KK if insolvency/creditor facts emerge, KKS if invoice/VAT irregularities exist, and UOKiK if there is a collective consumer pattern.

# C. Procedure — filing and beyond

## C1. Where to file

You can notify either the **police** or the **prosecutor**. The official government guidance says to prepare documents, go to the police or prosecutor, and either make an oral report recorded in a protocol or submit a written notice; the service is free. ([Gov.pl][9])

For this case, I would file directly with the **territorially competent Prokuratura Rejonowa** for the debtor’s registered/business address in Kraków, with a copy or optional parallel filing to the police only if your lawyer thinks it useful. Filing with the prosecutor is cleaner because this is economic-crime/fraud/creditor-crime analysis, not a simple police incident.

## C2. Form of the filing

Title the document:

**Zawiadomienie o podejrzeniu popełnienia przestępstwa wraz z wnioskiem o ściganie, wnioskami dowodowymi, wnioskiem o zabezpieczenie majątkowe oraz wnioskiem o naprawienie szkody**

Meaning:

**Notice of suspected offence, together with motion for prosecution, evidence motions, motion for asset security, and motion for repair of damage.**

A written notice should include the authority, your name/address, description of the event, justification, date, and signature. Prosecutor guidance also states that a notice may be sent by post, delivered in person, or submitted orally for a protocol. ([Gov.pl][10])

Because Polish criminal procedural writings generally require reliable signature/form compliance, the safest route is:

1. Polish lawyer drafts it in Polish;
2. you sign, or the lawyer signs under power of attorney;
3. send by registered mail / courier or file in person at the prosecutor’s office;
4. keep proof of filing and delivery.

Ordinary e-mail is risky for procedural filings. Some official pages say written notices can be sent by e-mail/fax/post, but prosecution-office guidance also warns that criminal procedural writings requiring a handwritten signature are not effectively filed by ordinary email, and ePUAP rules differ by unit. ([Gov.pl][9])

## C3. Attachments

Attach, in indexed order:

1. proof of payment of 155,000 PLN;
2. order confirmation / contract / terms and conditions;
3. invoice, pro-forma, advance invoice, or VAT document;
4. correspondence about delivery delays;
5. written refund agreement or refund promise;
6. your demand for payment and proof of delivery;
7. EPU filing confirmation and case number Nc-e 552126/26;
8. debtor’s email about Hong Kong supplier and “bankruptcy protection”;
9. CEIDG extract;
10. VAT white-list printout;
11. KRZ no-entry search proof;
12. website screenshots and archived pages;
13. any public evidence of similar complaints, clearly labelled as “investigative leads, not proven facts.”

## C4. Language

Polish is the practical filing language. If you file in English, the authority may understand it as a notice, but the case will move more efficiently if the complaint and key attachments are in Polish.

KPK provides for an interpreter when a person who does not speak Polish is examined and for translation of foreign-language documents where necessary. ([Isap][2])

The Polish Ombudsman and police guidance also confirm that a non-Polish-speaking person being examined should have an interpreter and should not bear that interpreter cost. ([Bip RPO][11])

## C5. Your procedural status as *pokrzywdzony*

A *pokrzywdzony* is a natural or legal person whose legal interest has been directly violated or threatened by an offence. ([Isap][2])

As victim/injured party in preparatory proceedings, you can:

* submit evidence motions;
* participate in certain acts;
* appoint an attorney;
* request file access;
* appeal refusals or discontinuance;
* request restitution;
* later act as *oskarżyciel posiłkowy* — auxiliary prosecutor — if an indictment is filed. ([Isap][2])

## C6. Restitution: art. 46 KK, not classic adhesive civil claim

The modern practical mechanism is not a classic *powództwo adhezyjne*. The former adhesive civil-claim provisions were removed from the Criminal Procedure Code in 2015. Use a **motion under art. 46 KK** instead.

Art. 46 KK allows the criminal court, upon conviction and on the injured party’s motion, to order repair of damage or compensation. ([Isap][3])

KPK art. 49a allows the victim or prosecutor to submit the art. 46 KK motion up to the close of the main trial. ([Isap][2])

## C7. Decision to open or refuse investigation

If there is justified suspicion of an offence, the authority initiates investigation. Anyone aware of an ex officio offence has a social duty to notify the prosecutor or police. ([Isap][2])

After receiving a notice, the authority should act immediately. If verification is needed, *postępowanie sprawdzające* can be used, with a decision to open or refuse generally within 30 days. ([Isap][2])

If you are not notified within 6 weeks about opening or refusing proceedings, you can complain to the superior prosecutor or supervisory organ. ([Isap][2])

## C8. Appeal against refusal or discontinuance

If the prosecutor refuses to initiate or discontinues the case, the injured party can file **zażalenie**. KPK art. 306 provides appeal rights to the injured party against refusal/discontinuance. ([Isap][2])

The general deadline for a *zażalenie* is usually **7 days** from service or announcement of the decision. ([Sip Lex][12])

A good *zażalenie* should not just argue “this is unfair.” It should identify missed investigative steps, such as failure to obtain bank records, failure to verify the Hong Kong supplier, failure to check other customers, failure to secure accounting/VAT records, or premature classification as a civil dispute.

## C9. Physical presence in Poland / remote participation

A witness generally has a duty to appear and testify, but KPK permits remote audio-video witness examination. In preparatory proceedings, the prosecutor ensures the technical conditions. ([Isap][2])

Because you do not live in Poland year-round, request remote examination with an interpreter. A lawyer can handle filings, appeals, and file access, but cannot fully replace your factual witness testimony.

## C10. Recommended investigative requests to include

Ask the prosecutor to:

1. obtain the debtor’s bank records from the date of your payment onward;
2. trace whether your 155,000 PLN was transferred to the alleged Hong Kong supplier;
3. identify the supplier, contract, invoice, and delivery status;
4. verify whether the supplier existed and when it allegedly collapsed;
5. check whether the debtor was already insolvent when taking your money;
6. secure accounting records, invoices, VAT/JPK records, order book, supplier correspondence;
7. identify other customers who paid and did not receive goods/refunds;
8. examine CEIDG/VAT/KRZ status and the claimed “bankruptcy protection”;
9. consider KPK asset security under arts. 291–295;
10. if fiscal irregularities appear, notify or coordinate with KAS.

# D. Costs to you

## D1. Filing fee

Filing a crime notice is **free**. The government crime-reporting page states that reporting is free of charge. ([Gov.pl][9])

## D2. Lawyer costs

Official minimum legal-fee regulations are much lower than actual market pricing. The official minimums include 540 PLN for representation in *dochodzenie* and 900 PLN for *śledztwo*, and they apply accordingly to attorneys for the injured party / auxiliary prosecutor. ([Isap][13])

Practical market estimates for a case like this:

| Work                                              | Realistic private-market estimate |
| ------------------------------------------------- | --------------------------------: |
| Initial legal review and strategy                 |                     500–2,000 PLN |
| Draft criminal complaint with evidence index      |                   2,000–8,000 PLN |
| Representation as *pokrzywdzony* in investigation |                 4,000–12,000+ PLN |
| Appeal against refusal / discontinuance           |                   1,500–5,000 PLN |
| Auxiliary prosecutor participation at trial       |                 8,000–30,000+ PLN |
| Hourly rates for commercial/criminal lawyers      |           often 300–750+ PLN/hour |

Treat these as market estimates, not statutory fees.

## D3. Translation costs

Official sworn-translator rates for work requested by courts/prosecutors/police are set per translation page. A page is 1,125 characters. The official rate for English/German/French/Russian into Polish is 44.07 PLN per page; from Polish into those languages it is 57.65 PLN per page. Urgent, specialist, handwritten, or poor-quality documents can increase the rate. ([Dziennik Ustaw][14])

Private sworn translation is often higher. For a practical budget, assume **55–100+ PLN per sworn page**, more for urgent or technical material.

## D4. Travel and time

Likely time burden:

| Phase                                    |                            Your likely time |
| ---------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------: |
| Evidence organisation                    |                                  4–10 hours |
| Lawyer intake / timeline review          |                                   2–5 hours |
| Complaint review/signature               |                                   1–3 hours |
| Witness statement                        |                  1–4 hours, possibly remote |
| Follow-up evidence / appeals             |                                  3–15 hours |
| Trial participation if indictment occurs | highly variable; potentially multiple dates |

Travel to Poland may be avoidable if remote examination is accepted, but not guaranteed. KPK permits remote witness examination, but the authority controls whether and how it is used. ([Isap][2])

## D5. Hidden costs

Expect possible costs for:

* sworn translation of selected key emails/documents;
* certified copies;
* courier/registered mail;
* lawyer review of prosecutor correspondence;
* lawyer attendance at witness examination;
* translations of court/prosecutor decisions into English;
* travel if remote testimony is refused.

A lean initial filing can be done for a few thousand PLN. A fully lawyer-led criminal track through appeal/trial can become tens of thousands of PLN.

# E. Consequences for the debtor

## E1. Sentencing exposure

For **art. 286 § 1 KK**, the statutory exposure is **6 months to 8 years’ imprisonment**. ([Isap][3])

For a single-victim 155,000 PLN merchant case with no prior record, the realistic outcome if convicted is more likely to involve:

* restitution under art. 46 KK;
* fine;
* possibly imprisonment in a range that may be eligible for suspension;
* criminal record;
* possible business ban if the court finds the business activity was abused.

Suspension is legally possible where the imposed imprisonment does not exceed 1 year, the offender was not previously sentenced to imprisonment, and the court considers suspension sufficient. ([Isap][3])

Actual imprisonment becomes more realistic if there are multiple victims, high aggregate loss, prior convictions, concealment of assets, false documents, continued fraud after insolvency, obstruction, or no restitution.

## E2. Business ban

The Criminal Code allows a court to prohibit a profession, position, or business activity where the offender abused that activity or continued activity threatens legally protected interests. ([Isap][3])

Bankruptcy Law has a more direct business-ban mechanism: the bankruptcy court may ban a culpable debtor for 1–10 years from business activity and management/representative functions where the person failed to file for bankruptcy in time or committed other specified insolvency misconduct. ([Isap][5])

## E3. Restitution to you

On conviction, art. 46 KK allows the court to order repair of damage. On your motion, the court should order it if the statutory conditions are met. ([Isap][3])

This does not mean immediate payment. It gives you an enforceable restitution obligation after the criminal judgment is final.

## E4. Asset freeze / *zabezpieczenie majątkowe*

This is the most strategically valuable criminal-procedure feature, but it is **not automatic**.

KPK art. 291 permits asset security on the accused’s property if the accused is charged with an offence for which a fine, forfeiture, compensation, damage repair, return of benefit, or similar measure may be ordered, and there is a justified fear that enforcement will be impossible or significantly hindered. ([Isap][2])

KPK arts. 293–295 regulate the prosecutor’s/court’s security orders and appeal rights. ([Isap][2])

Practical meaning:

* filing a complaint does not freeze assets;
* opening checking proceedings does not freeze assets automatically;
* a freeze becomes realistic only if the prosecutor treats the case seriously, identifies suspect-level facts, and sees execution risk;
* you should still request it clearly and support it with non-refund, ignored civil demand, EPU filing, alleged insolvency, and possible lack of KRZ confirmation.

## E5. Pressure value

A criminal proceeding can create real burden for the debtor:

* police/prosecutor summons;
* obligation to explain supplier story;
* bank-record tracing;
* accounting-record review;
* possible search/seizure;
* KAS coordination;
* reputational concern;
* lawyer costs;
* risk of restitution order, criminal record, and business ban.

But you cannot control the prosecutor once the complaint is filed, and you should not use the criminal process as a private bargaining weapon.

# F. Leverage and strategic value

## F1. Will filing cause the debtor to engage or settle?

There is no reliable public statistic for “merchant prepayment fraud complaints under 200,000 PLN causing settlement.” The practical creditor experience is mixed.

A criminal complaint can create leverage if the debtor is merely stalling, has assets, or fears scrutiny. It has lower leverage if the debtor is genuinely insolvent and the facts look like a failed supply transaction.

The main leverage comes from the state’s ability to obtain evidence you cannot obtain privately: bank records, accounting documents, supplier verification, and evidence of other victims.

## F2. Bankruptcy interaction

A pending criminal investigation does not automatically block consumer bankruptcy.

A final criminal judgment can matter much more. Bankruptcy Law makes certain obligations non-dischargeable, including obligations to repair damage resulting from an offence or misdemeanor established by a final judgment, and penal/probation compensation-type obligations. ([Isap][5])

In consumer bankruptcy, the court may refuse a repayment plan/discharge where the bankrupt intentionally caused or materially increased insolvency, unless equity or humanitarian reasons justify otherwise. ([Isap][5])

## F3. Ethical and legal limits on using criminal complaints as leverage

Polish law treats unlawful threats seriously. Art. 191 KK penalises using violence or unlawful threat to force another person to act, omit, or endure something. ([Sip Lex][15])

The definition of unlawful threat includes a threat to cause criminal or administrative proceedings, but there is an important carve-out: it is not an unlawful threat to announce proceedings if the announcement is intended solely to protect a right violated by an offence or administrative wrong. ([Isap][3])

Safe approach:

* file a truthful, good-faith complaint;
* do not say “pay me or I will accuse you”;
* do not offer to withdraw or suppress criminal allegations in exchange for payment;
* let a Polish lawyer handle settlement wording;
* if mentioning criminal proceedings, use neutral wording: “I have filed / will file a notice with competent authorities because I believe the facts require investigation.”

# G. Risks and downsides to you

## G1. False accusation — art. 234 KK

Art. 234 KK penalises falsely accusing another person before an authority of committing an offence, fiscal offence, misdemeanor, or disciplinary wrong. Penalty: 3 months–5 years. ([Isap][3])

Your risk is low if you:

* state only facts you can prove;
* label uncertain points as suspicions;
* request investigation rather than asserting guilt;
* attach documents;
* avoid exaggeration;
* do not fabricate or selectively distort evidence.

Your risk increases if you knowingly allege facts that are false.

## G2. Defamation / GDPR / reputational risk

Do not publish accusations online. Send evidence to your lawyer, prosecutor, police, UOKiK, KAS, or court. Public CEIDG data does not give you a free pass to publish unproven criminal allegations.

If you search for other victims, collect only public information lawfully available and label it as leads, not proven facts.

## G3. Your own conduct

Your conduct does not obviously create criminal exposure from the facts provided. But the authorities may ask:

* whether the purchase was personal or business;
* why 155,000 PLN was prepaid;
* what representations induced payment;
* whether you received any partial performance;
* what refund terms were agreed;
* whether your EPU claim statements match your criminal complaint.

Consistency matters.

## G4. Impact on civil case

A criminal complaint does not normally stop the civil claim. In some cases, a civil court may suspend proceedings if criminal findings could materially affect the civil judgment, but that is not automatic. For a straightforward payment/refund claim, the civil court can often proceed independently.

## G5. Stress and time

The main downsides are delay, procedural complexity, translation, and the possibility that the prosecutor dismisses the case as a civil dispute. That is why the complaint must be built around investigative leads showing possible deception, insolvency, asset movement, or a multi-victim pattern.

# H. Coordination with the civil track

## H1. Recommended sequencing

Proceed **in parallel**:

1. continue EPU/civil claim;
2. file criminal complaint now;
3. request criminal asset security;
4. after any civil judgment/enforcement steps, update the prosecutor if the debtor still does not pay or appears to hide assets;
5. consider Bankruptcy Law art. 373/374 business-ban strategy if insolvency evidence develops.

## H2. Civil security vs. criminal security

Criminal asset security does not substitute neatly for civil security. It can secure fines, forfeiture, restitution, and damage-repair obligations, but it depends on the prosecutor/court and suspect status. KPK art. 291 requires justified fear that enforcement will be impossible or hindered. ([Isap][2])

Civil security remains your own tool. Criminal security is a potentially powerful supplement if the prosecutor acts quickly.

## H3. What to tell the prosecutor about the civil case

Include the civil case, but frame it carefully:

* “I am pursuing civil recovery.”
* “The civil claim does not resolve whether the debtor intentionally misled me or is frustrating creditors.”
* “The debtor’s supplier/bankruptcy explanation requires investigative powers I do not possess.”
* “The risk of asset dissipation justifies considering KPK art. 291 security.”

# I. Realistic outcomes and base-rate evidence

## I1. Public statistics

Public police data exists for art. 286 KK in aggregate. The police provide annual data for proceedings initiated and offences confirmed under art. 286 KK for 1999–2023. ([Statystyka][16])

The 2023 spreadsheet result shows 105,682 initiated proceedings and 146,553 confirmed offences under art. 286 KK, but this is aggregate fraud data and does not isolate merchant prepayment cases, amounts under 200,000 PLN, dismissal rates, or conviction rates. ([Statystyka][17])

The Prokuratura Krajowa publishes statistical reports, including 2025 reports, but I did not find a public slice answering your exact base-rate question: “single-victim merchant prepayment art. 286 cases under 200,000 PLN.” ([Gov.pl][18])

## I2. Typical reasons for refusal or discontinuance

In similar contract/fraud boundary cases, common reasons for refusal or discontinuance are:

* no proof of fraudulent intent at contract formation;
* evidence shows failed business transaction rather than deception;
* debtor did make some supplier arrangements;
* debtor’s insolvency occurred after the contract;
* no evidence funds were diverted;
* single-victim case with no broader pattern;
* prosecutor concludes the civil court is the proper forum.

The Supreme Court’s 2025 reasoning is important here: non-performance and non-refund are not enough by themselves. ([Supreme Court][1])

## I3. Expected timeline

Practical timeline:

| Stage                                 | Realistic expectation                        |
| ------------------------------------- | -------------------------------------------- |
| Filing                                | immediate once document is signed and filed  |
| Checking proceedings / initial review | up to about 30 days if verification needed   |
| No-response complaint trigger         | if no notice within 6 weeks                  |
| Refusal/discontinuance appeal         | usually 7 days from service                  |
| Investigation if opened               | months; sometimes longer                     |
| Indictment/trial/verdict              | often 1–3+ years in contested economic cases |

The 30-day checking-proceeding rule and 6-week no-response complaint mechanism are grounded in KPK provisions. ([Isap][2])
